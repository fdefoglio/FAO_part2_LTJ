[0] # Colombia’s Multipurpose Cadastre: Navigating Technical Innovation, Social Inclusion, and Intercultural Recognition

[1] # Abstract

[2] This paper critically examines Colombia’s multipurpose cadastre policy, focusing on three key dimensions: community participation, gender inclusion, and Indigenous rights. It identifies opportunities for legitimacy and efficiency through participatory methods—such as social mapping—and for advancing women’s visibility as “interested parties” in cadastral databases. Yet, major barriers to guarantee social inclusion persist, including technical centralization, high costs of engagement, administrative complexity, and entrenched patriarchal norms.

[3] However, Decree 0462 of 2025 issued by the National government, resulting from a four-year prior consultation with Indigenous Peoples, illustrates how cadastre reform can align with Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure (VGGT) principles. By recognizing the multidimensionality of territories, validating ancestral law, and enabling Indigenous cadastral operators, the decree offers lessons on intercultural governance. The Decree and the challenges that persist in cadastral operations reveal both the potential and the limitations of Colombia’s cadastre to advance inclusive and equitable land tenure.

[4] Keywords: VGGT, multipurpose cadastre, participation, gender, Indigenous Peoples, Colombia

[5] # 1. Introduction

[6] This paper demonstrates that participatory mechanisms within cadastral processes are diverse and play a critical role in achieving legitimacy, social inclusion, and long‑term sustainability. Despite their importance, significant technical, institutional and cultural barriers continue to limit their effective implementation. To examine these dynamics, the analysis focuses on two differentiated approaches that illustrate both the potential and the challenges of inclusion in cadastral governance. The first concerns the gender approach, particularly the inclusion of women, where substantial opportunities for improvement persist. The second relates to prior consultation processes, which have led to the adoption of a decree aimed at reconciling the Western cadastral framework with the ancestral and traditional worldviews of Indigenous Peoples.

[7] The paper is structured as follows. The first section situates the multipurpose cadastre within Colombia’s broader policy framework and peacebuilding agenda. This is followed by an analysis of participatory mechanisms in cadastral processes, highlighting their contribution to legitimacy and social inclusion, as well as the barriers that constrain their effective implementation. Subsequent sections examine the two differentiated approaches in greater depth: first, gender inclusion within the multipurpose cadastre, and second, prior consultation and intercultural governance in Indigenous territories.

[8] This paper offers a critical and practice-based reflection on Colombia’s multipurpose cadastre policy, with a particular focus on mechanisms for civic participation, the integration of a gender-sensitive approach, and recent normative advancements in Indigenous territories. Rather than limiting itself to a conceptual framework, the analysis is grounded in the author’s direct involvement in the design, implementation, and coordination of participatory instruments within the scope of this policy.

[9] Accordingly, this paper is situated at the intersection of applied research and the systematization of professional practice, aiming to contribute to academic debates and inform public policy formulation on inclusive land governance in Colombia. The following sections address, in sequence, the structural context of land inequality and the Peace Agreement, the role of participation and social inclusion in the cadastre, gender dynamics, intercultural governance in Indigenous territories, the institutional framework, and conclude with policy recommendations.

[10] # 2. Multipurpose cadastre, Peace Agreement, and land inequality

[11] Following the 2016 Peace Agreement—and responding to a long-standing need identified by scholars analyzing Colombia’s armed conflict—a public policy and jurisprudential instrument was consolidated that highlighted the importance of updating information on land tenure to advance the Comprehensive Rural Reform (RRI). The aim of the RRI is to close longstanding rural–urban gaps, eradicate rural poverty and hunger, promote equality, and ensure the full enjoyment of rights for rural populations, while addressing structural drivers of conflict such as unequal access to land, insecure tenure, and the historical neglect of rural territories by the state.

[12] Colombia has a bimodal land structure (Machado, 2009), characterized by a highly unequal distribution in which a small number of landholders control large estates while the majority own very limited areas. This pattern has been shaped over several decades by the historical orientation of agrarian policy, including early reformist efforts such as Law 200 of 1936, which recognized the social function of property but failed to achieve substantial land redistribution. The persistence of this structure has restricted access to land and productive opportunities for large segments of the rural population, contributing to sustained rural poverty and, indirectly, to urban poverty through rural-to-urban migration and labour market exclusion (Fajardo, 2002: 73).

[13] This structural inequality is reflected in Colombia’s land tenure system, where the land-ownership Gini coefficient reached 0.89 in 2025, one of the highest globally. Rural poverty remains deeply rooted in this context: multidimensional poverty stood at 22.4%—more than three times the urban rate—and rural monetary poverty exceeded 40% (Colombia’s National Statistics Department -DANE-, 2025). These outcomes coincide with an extremely unequal agrarian structure, in which less than 2% of landowners control nearly 44% of rural land (IGAC, 2023), alongside widespread land-tenure informality affecting more than half of rural territory (UPRA, 2025). Gender disparities further compound this situation, as only around 36% of rural plots are registered in women’s names, and women’s landholdings tend to be smaller and of lower quality, reflecting a persistent structural gender gap in access to and control over land (DANE, 2022).

[14] By contrast, although lands formally recognized as belonging to Indigenous Peoples and Afro-Colombian communities account for approximately 34–36% of the national territory, the lack of cadastral updating in collective territories generates cumulative effects on legal security, territorial autonomy, public planning, and the differentiated protection of rights.

[15] From a VGGT perspective, the coexistence of highly concentrated private landholdings and extensive collective territories in Colombia poses distinct challenges for tenure governance. While gender inequalities in private land access and control conflict with VGGT principles of equality and non-discrimination, the main challenge in collective territories lies in strengthening tenure security through updated cadastral information, despite formal recognition of collective rights.

[16] In the context of persistent inequalities, limited rights recognition, and social tensions, the cadastre is a key instrument for advancing the objectives of the Comprehensive Rural Reform (RRI) and aligning with the VGGT. By identifying tenure arrangements, land occupancy, and landholdings, it enables more informed, equitable, and culturally appropriate agrarian policy decisions, underscoring the importance of participation and inclusion to ensure outcomes that genuinely contribute to peace.

[22] # 3. Participation and social inclusion in the cadastre

[23] With the structural context established, it becomes clear that the effectiveness of the multipurpose cadastre depends not only on technical innovation and policy alignment, but also on the meaningful involvement of local communities and stakeholders. Participation emerges as a cornerstone for social inclusion, legitimacy, and sustainability within cadastral processes.

[24] In the 21st century—following the enactment of the 1991 Colombian Constitution and the signing of the Peace Agreement in 2016—there is no longer a debate over whether participation is important; rather, the key questions now are: Participation for what? Is it simply for information-sharing? For negotiation and agreement? For governance? For capacity-building? Who should participate? Under what conditions? In other words: what is the scope of participation in the cadastre considering prevailing technical and legal standards?

[25] As a starting point, one may ask: if cadastral information serves as the basis for decision-making on territorial matters—such as environmental management, spatial planning, land formalization, and taxation—how can it be ensured that this information genuinely represents the territory? A first and essential response is that all people who inhabit the land must be involved. This requires diverse and inclusive mechanisms that enable individuals—including women, Indigenous Peoples, and Afro-descendant communities—to contribute information about the places where they live. Such participation can also function as a form of social oversight, representing those who are temporarily absent from their land for reasons such as forced displacement. Through these processes, locally grounded knowledge can be translated into graphical, numerical, and textual representations that inform state decision-making and shape policies that directly affect people’s lives.

[26] ## 3.1 Exchange of information

[27] Participation takes multiple forms within the cadastral process. At first glance, one of the most basic forms is the exchange of information. If people do not understand the purpose of the cadastre, it becomes difficult to expect them to provide data about their land plots—which, in many cases, are deeply tied to their identity and livelihoods. Recent events in different municipalities of Colombia, where citizens openly expressed dissatisfaction with cadastral updates due to increased property taxes, illustrate the importance of effective and transparent communication with the public.

[28] It is not advisable to conclude a cadastral formation or updating process without ensuring that all relevant stakeholders are adequately informed, understand the outcomes of the cadastral identification process, and have access to the resulting data. This includes women, Indigenous Peoples, and Afro-descendant communities, which in turn requires the use of differentiated and culturally appropriate engagement mechanisms. Ideally, by the end of any cadastral intervention, residents should be able to recognize themselves—and their neighbours—in the cadastral databases, understand how the information was generated, and know how it will be used by the State.

[29] ## 3.2 Meaningful community inclusion in cadastral operations

[30] Advancing this participatory approach requires more than simply providing information, awareness raising, or organizing outreach activities. It is essential to ensure meaningful community inclusion in cadastral operations and processes. Only then can ownership of the process be achieved, avoiding surprises, rework, complaints, legal claims, and unnecessary waste of resources. Local communities must feel that they are active participants in the registration of land information. This implies going beyond informational meetings. This approach requires the coordinated scheduling of visits to land parcels or data-collection activities with local stakeholders, ensuring the participation of women even when they are not formally recognised as legal owners or do not self-identify as such.

[31] Equally important is the inclusion of the local community in a public display or review of the final cadastral survey outputs for a given territory. Such an approach constitutes a highly efficient and effective mechanism for verification and quality control of cadastral information, as it relies on a form of democratic and cost-effective “crowdsourcing”.

[32] Achieving this form of meaningful participation entails a set of technical and institutional conditions that extend well beyond fieldlevel engagement. It requires strong interinstitutional coordination to ensure access to complete and consistent parcellevel information, as well as sustained efforts to build community capacities to understand the objectives and implications of land surveys and cadastral data collection. Moreover, contractual frameworks must be designed to allow sufficient time, flexibility, and guarantees for technical teams to meet deadlines while engaging effectively with communities through meetings, followup actions, and trustbuilding activities. Implementing cadastral operations in this manner is considerably more complex—and initially more resourceintensive—than contracting survey crews to visit parcels or relying primarily on remotesensing analysis.

[33] In practice, unforeseen challenges frequently arise, further complicating compliance with operational targets and timelines. However, from a sustainability perspective, investing in participatory approaches can reduce downstream costs associated with conflicts, rework, legal disputes, litigation and loss of data credibility, reinforcing participation as a strategic investment rather than an operational burden.

[34] ## 3.3 Community-Based participation in cadastral data collection

[35] Another form of participation relates to the challenges of collecting data in complex geographies, such as Colombia’s mountainous terrain, rivers, forests, and remote areas, which often render aerial imagery insufficient and make on-the-ground surveying costly, time-consuming, and logistically difficult. In addition, some areas present serious security risks, making it nearly impossible for external personnel to access them. In such contexts, the only people who know the territory because they live there and have walked it are residents themselves. While this may seem obvious, there are still not appropriate mechanisms in place to enable communities to carry out this task effectively.

[36] One of the main reasons why cadastral authorities and operators face difficulties in enabling community participation in cadastral processes lies in the high level of complexity of existing data-collection formats, as well as in the technical procedures required to process the information once it has been gathered. These procedures typically demand specialised knowledge of information systems and digital tools, making it virtually impossible for residents without technical training to undertake such tasks. As a result, cadastral surveying continues to rely almost exclusively on professionals trained in land surveys, topography, geography, and land administration to collect georeferenced data within defined territories.

[37] This challenge is further compounded by the limited availability of such professionals in Colombia. According to a study by the National Planning Department (DNP, 2026), the most significant capacity gaps within the cadastral value chain are found among cartographers and cadastral and topographic engineers—where demand is estimated at 1,211 professionals compared to an available supply of only 449—as well as land-recognition technicians (reconocedores prediales, property inspectors), with an estimated demand of 732 professionals and a supply of only six. These shortages raise serious concerns regarding the feasibility and timelines for meeting the cadastral-related targets established in the Peace Agreement.

[38] Participation can play a crucial role in addressing constraints such as shortages of qualified professionals, security risks, and limited access to areas with complex or rugged topography, particularly when participatory mechanisms are designed to incorporate technical capacity-building components. If more accessible technical-administrative procedures were developed, these could support communities in collecting the territorial information required for the cadastre.

[39] ## 3.4 Indirect methods: strengthening collective work

[40] In addition to designing participatory methods that enable communities to collect data directly in the field, it is also important to consider indirect methods, which open the door to highly valuable tools for mapping a territory using deeply participatory and inclusive methodologies, among them: social cartography. This is a tool that allows for the gathering of information about the social representation of the territory, identifying where and how communities interact with, and assign meaning to, space.

[41] Indirect methods, such as the use of images, facilitate the implementation of social cartography, thereby reshaping how spatial surveys are conducted. Rather than relying exclusively on field-based measurements, this approach makes use of Geographic Information Systems (GIS) to analyse territory through digital imagery. However, GIS does not eliminate fieldwork; instead, it reconfigures it. The focus shifts away from physically walking parcel boundaries toward collective validation exercises that corroborate information derived from imagery and digital data, while also capturing territorial knowledge that remote sensing alone cannot reveal. In these contexts, the availability of strategies that actively facilitate the participation of women, Indigenous Peoples, and other population groups is essential to ensure the accuracy and reliability of cadastral information.

[42] ## 3.5 Participation, institutional design, and the sustainability of cadastral operations

[43] A fundamental question in ensuring the various forms that participation and inclusion may take throughout the cadastral process is: To what extent are cadastral authorities, governmental institutions, and operators willing to make the necessary investments to enable a truly participatory and inclusive process? It is essential that these actors weigh the decision between allocating sufficient resources to promote participation and inclusion during the operation and the social, political, and economic costs of failing to do so. The absence of adequate participation and inclusion generates conditions for conflict with local and national institutions, undermines public trust in the state and the authoritativeness of cadastral information, and often leads to reactive expenditures, such as revisiting technical procedures, organising ad hoc dialogues, and making post-process adjustments in response to public dissatisfaction.

[44] This calls for a rethinking of how cadastral operations are financially designed, ensuring that a strong participatory component is included from the outset. It is worth noting that Resolution 1040 of 2023, issued by IGAC, includes participation as a cross-cutting component. However, it leaves the mechanisms and forms of participation and inclusion open to interpretation, placing the burden of implementation on individual actors.

[45] Another obstacle lies in the complexity of the technical-administrative procedures required to promote participation, which can hinder community involvement in data collection efforts. As institutional processes grow more complex, citizens’ ability to engage meaningfully tends to diminish. This is not to suggest that local communities can replace the specialised knowledge of cadastral, geographic, or land surveying professionals. Rather, the point is that, under current conditions, participation and efficiency could complement one another, enabling faster, more legitimate data collection processes.

[46] What is needed is a detailed, technical analysis of how to effectively promote citizen participation in ways that generate useful data and simultaneously improve the legitimacy, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness of cadastral operations. Consistent with the Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure (VGGT), local participation can accelerate cadastral surveys and updates, improve data quality, and reduce overall costs, while simultaneously strengthen local capacities for land administration. By building a local cadre of trained para-surveyors and land-related para-legal actors, participation supports the long-term maintenance of cadastral information. Without sustained maintenance, the value of cadastral data as a public asset rapidly deteriorates, undermining the effectiveness of initial investments.

[47] The cadastre is a methodology, a policy instrument, but above all, it is a crucial source of information for decision-making on territorial administration. That information must be accurate, transparent, and accessible to the public, who should be able to understand it and support it. Otherwise, any decisions based on that information will be subject to scepticism and resistance due to insufficient authoritativeness of the information.

[48] Finally, it is vital to underscore that participatory and inclusive mechanisms contribute directly to the sustainability of cadastral information. One of the main challenges in implementing this policy lies in how to ensure that, after significant investment of time and resources in each operation, the information remains accurate over time. The answer, once again, lies with the communities themselves. It is essential that they understand the value of the information, know how to report mutations in their parcels, and that the mechanisms for doing so are efficient, accessible, and transparent. This is not a task to be undertaken after the cadastral operation concludes—rather, the operation must lay the groundwork for sustainability from the outset.

[49] Although significant efforts have been made at the institutional level to strengthen participation, in practice, communities in areas where cadastral updating has taken place continue to express dissatisfaction, raising questions and concerns about the effectiveness of these processes (El Tiempo, 2024; Mutantes TV, 2023; Vanguardia, 2025). It is worth highlighting the experience of the Intercultural School (IGAC, n.d.), which represents a major initiative to build local community capacities. However, this effort has not always been aligned with the locations where cadastral operations are carried out, as it is not mandated by regulations requiring cadastral operators to implement it.

[50] While participatory and inclusive mechanisms are fundamental for the legitimacy and sustainability of the multipurpose cadastre, it is equally essential to address the specific forms of exclusion that persist within these processes. Among these, gender-based inequalities remain particularly pronounced, shaping both access to land and the capacity to participate meaningfully in cadastral operations. The following chapter examines the gender dynamics within the multipurpose cadastre, highlighting the structural barriers faced by women and exploring strategies to advance gender equality and non-discrimination in land governance.

[51] ## 3.6 Gender and the Multipurpose Cadastre

[52] While participatory mechanisms are fundamental for social inclusion, they must be complemented by differentiated approaches that address structural gender inequalities in land governance. In Colombia, women account for 51.2% of the population, constituting a demographic majority that has nevertheless been historically discriminated against and excluded from multiple spheres of public life and, consequently, from the benefits of public policy interventions.

[53] According to DANE, which recently integrated and compared databases to characterise women’s land tenure in rural areas, 63.7 percent of individually owned properties are registered in men’s names, while only 36.3 percent are registered in women’s names. In addition, jointly registered properties account for 47.4 percent. This disparity undermines women’s ability to participate in decision-making processes on equal terms. Gender gaps in landownership stem from several deeply rooted factors, such as male-biased inheritance practices, the privileged legal status of men in marriage, gender bias in both community-based and state-run land distribution programmes, and male favouritism in land markets (Deere & Léon, 2003; DANE, 2022).

[54] Traditional and customary norms in Latin America—deeply ingrained in Colombian culture—validate men as the legitimate subjects for managing property, controlling finances, making business decisions, and publicly representing the family. This indirectly assigns women a secondary role in decision-making, both within the household and in the public sphere (Zorio, 2016).

[55] A key factor underlying this patriarchal worldview is the lack of recognition—or outright invisibility—of the essential contributions women make through unpaid care work, now referred to as the care economy. These contributions are vast and multifaceted, including caring for children and elderly family members, gathering firewood or charcoal for cooking, tending animals, managing household gardens and food crops, and cooking and feeding day labourers working on the land. Despite this extensive and indispensable labour, traditional rural economies continue to erase women's contributions from agricultural production and from the well-being of families and communities. As a result, women are perceived as non-productive dependents, and their labour is undervalued and labelled merely as “help” rather than economic work (Zorio, 2016: 298).

[56] For these and other reasons, FAO (2022) has identified a series of challenges that must be addressed to promote the realization of rural women’s land rights. These include ensuring the periodic and timely updating of cadastral and property registration records in response to new informal events (such as unregistered sales), fully guaranteeing social participation in the Comprehensive Rural Reform (RRI) during both its implementation and monitoring stages, and actively including rural women in documenting narratives and evaluations related to RRI implementation.

[57] In line with these challenges, the multipurpose cadastre plays a central role, as it enables the visibility of women within cadastral databases, which is essential to identifying land parcels that can be prioritized for formalizing women’s land titles, particularly those classified as public lands (baldíos) or those where women are listed as interested parties. This task falls under the purview of the National Land Agency. Similarly, the cadastre can help identify areas with a high presence of women where productive development initiatives may be prioritized—a responsibility of the Rural Development Agency. These are just a few examples of how cadastral data can serve as a crucial input for informed and inclusive public policy decisions.

[58] Being classified as an ‘interested party’ in cadastre is not equivalent to being the owner, holder, or occupier of a land parcel. These categories require legal analysis of land tenure status, which is a highly complex task and, among other things, the responsibility of the National Land Agency, not of the multipurpose cadastre itself. However, the initial classification made within cadastral databases (based on criteria that should be publicly available) plays a significant role in determining whether the individuals who inhabit a parcel are registered as potential owners, possessors, or occupants.

[59] Identifying ‘interested parties’ under conditions of possession and occupation is far more complex. It requires information about who believes themselves to be the landowners, who works the land, and who resides there. Particular attention must be paid to ensuring that women are made visible during parcel identification, regardless of the method employed. One of the greatest challenges is that most women do not perceive themselves as landowners, possessors, or occupants. This stems from a patriarchal worldview whose strongest expression is the deeply entrenched and widespread machismo in Colombian society, especially pronounced in rural populations. As a result, communities -and women themselves- tend to assume that land belongs to their male partners, even though they also work and cultivate it.

[60] This patriarchal worldview is deeply embedded in Colombian society, and overcoming the barriers it imposes on women is a monumental task -one that permeates daily life and, naturally, the multipurpose cadastre-. Therefore, identifying those who consider themselves owners, who use or exploit the land, must be approached through a gender lens, recognizing women as key actors in the use of rural land and, consequently, as legitimate, deserving, and unequivocal interested parties in cadastral records.

[61] As part of the development of the variables comprising the cadastre survey, the male–female variable was finally introduced, marking a historic step toward having sex-disaggregated data in cadastral databases (based on national ID cards). However, this progress is not sufficient because addressing gender inequality in land rights requires a broad, deep, systematic, and sustained sensitization process about women’s rights to land and the multiple barriers they face in exercising those rights.

[62] Even if the gender variable is mandatory for cadastral operators a more analytical measure would be to incorporate a “suspicious category” into the quality control process. This means that if the representation of women in the cadastral database falls below a certain threshold (to be defined by quality control experts), it should trigger a review. Through sampling methods, quality control teams could verify whether information was collected for all interested parties on each parcel. If the number of female representations is “missing,” the database should be returned to the operator for correction and completion.

[63] Given the need for broad, deep, systematic, and sustained sensitization and training efforts, it is essential to develop a communications, training, and participation strategy that explicitly includes women. One way to ensure this is by incorporating a gender-sensitive approach into all previously mentioned participation mechanisms and into the communications strategy. This means ensuring that women are not only present in the cadastral operation but are also accurately represented in the databases and, consequently, within the Land Administration System.

[64] To this end, the strategy should include targeted messaging that explicitly invites women to participate in cadastral activities such as community meetings, parcel visits, and participatory mapping sessions (social cartography). It is also crucial to identify local women's organizations and establish strategic alliances with them to support the design and implementation of the cadastral operation. Moreover, communications should include messages that affirm and highlight the value of women’s contributions through the care economy, which remains largely invisible in land governance discussions.

[65] These actions should be complemented by training and awareness-raising spaces for cadastral implementation teams and decision-makers. This is particularly important because patriarchal worldviews are not limited to rural areas, they permeate society at large and manifest in multiple forms depending on the social, economic, and cultural context. These worldviews can bias the identification of “interested parties” in the cadastre, especially when it comes to recognizing women as rightful users or claimants of land.

[66] While addressing gender inequalities is essential for advancing inclusive land governance, it is equally important to recognise and respond to the distinct challenges faced by Indigenous Peoples and other traditional communities in the context of cadastral policy. The following section explores the intercultural dimensions of the multipurpose cadastre, focusing on prior consultation with Indigenous Peoples, normative innovations, and the articulation of Indigenous worldviews and rights within land administration processes.

[67] # 4. Indigenous Peoples and Intercultural Governance

[68] While the multipurpose cadastre faces the challenge of making gender inequalities visible and safeguarding rights in a field dominated by cultural, technical, and administrative logics, the case of Indigenous Peoples offers an even deeper lesson. Decree 0462 of 2025 issued by DANE, which regulates the implementation of this policy in Indigenous territories, illustrates how a legal instrument can become a space for intercultural dialogue, integrating human rights, cultural rights, and differential approaches. While the traditional state perspective rests on the premise that “land is a single entity, it is what can be measured,” Indigenous Peoples assert that land and territory are inseparable from life, origin, and identity. This contrast shows that, as with the gender agenda, the multipurpose cadastre only gains legitimacy when it recognizes and protects the multiple ways in which territory is understood and inhabited.

[69] The prior consultation with Indigenous Peoples for the implementation of the multipurpose cadastre extended over four years and four months (from December 2020 to April 2025, when the decree was issued). Although this duration may appear lengthy, it was shaped by political, regulatory, administrative, and operational tensions. Nevertheless, what matters is not the duration but the outcome: a regulatory instrument that reflects a series of technical agreements built through intercultural dialogue. Elements that might initially seem incompatible with a highly technical policy -such as the recognition of spirituality linked to territory, Indigenous normative systems (derecho propio), and collective governance structures- became the foundations of an innovative framework. This experience demonstrates how the principles of the VGGT can inform articulation between state-led cadastral modernization and Indigenous cosmovision, offering important lessons on the possibility of harmonizing approaches often considered irreconcilable in land governance.

[70] Several topics were successfully reconciled through dialogue, negotiation, and collective deliberation. First among them is the recognition that Indigenous territories cannot be reduced to parcels or land plots; rather, they encompass the multidimensionality of territory: the physical, the spiritual, the cultural, and the political. Thus, the decree incorporates definitions of ancestral use areas, sacred sites, and biocultural rights, which are central elements of Indigenous worldviews. These categories enable the multipurpose cadastre not only to record the material occupation of land but also to reflect the spiritual and cultural relationships that Indigenous Peoples maintain with their territories.

[71] A second illustrative example is the incorporation of the Law of Origin, Natural Law, and the Higher Law (Ley de Origen, Ley Natural y Derecho Mayor) as legitimate sources of law for the interpretation of the cadastre in Indigenous territories. This means that Indigenous normative systems are not treated as “complementary,” but rather as guiding principles for defining land uses, restrictions, and territorial management practices. In doing so, the multipurpose cadastre respects ancestral territorial governance and the authority of Indigenous councils and traditional authorities.

[72] The Decree also establishes that cadastral operators may be Indigenous, and that data collection must be conducted through prior agreement with Indigenous authorities. This ensures that the process is not an external imposition but rather an intercultural exercise in which the community validates the information and retains control over how its territory is represented. Furthermore, the decree provides for the creation of non-parcel layers to record spiritual or cultural information that cannot be translated into physical boundaries but is essential to Indigenous worldviews.

[73] Finally, the formalized prior consultation process between the Government and Indigenous organizations is reflected in safeguards such as cultural objection, the respect for multilingualism and Indigenous toponymy, and the creation of Indigenous-managed information systems administered by Indigenous institutions. These mechanisms ensure that the implementation of the multipurpose cadastre does not undermine Indigenous autonomy or spirituality. Instead, it becomes a tool that strengthens integral territoriality and the right to self-determination.

[74] To date, operations under this decree are ongoing, although none has yet been completed. Implementation remains a complex process that requires sustained coordination, extensive consultation, and significant resources. However, in the context of recent public controversies surrounding cadastral valuation and cadastral updating more broadly (Ramos Gámez, 2025; Portafolio, n.d.) the expressions of support voiced by some Indigenous Peoples’ organisations (Comisión Nacional de Territorios Indígenas [CNTI], n.d.; Organización Nacional Indígena de Colombia [ONIC], n.d) may be read as indicative of an emerging degree of policy legitimacy among certain Indigenous political actors.

[75] # 5. Conclusion

[76] The processes of participation and inclusion within the multipurpose cadastre should not be understood as isolated activities or one‑time events within cadastral operations, but rather as structural principles of public policy and of the Peace Agreement. In line with the Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure (VGGT), their effective guarantee requires the design of specific, systematic, and sustainable measures, grounded in rigorous technical, financial, and administrative implementation analyses.

[77] In this regard, participation is also “multipurpose”: it not only enhances efficiency and reduces costs in cadastral processes but also enables the exercise of a fundamental right and strengthens the legitimacy, transparency, and sustainability of the information produced, as emphasized by the VGGT. When the authoritative representation of territory fails to reflect the collective construction shaped by its inhabitants, decisions derived from it lack social grounding and are therefore more likely to be contested.

[78] Incorporating a gender perspective is essential to responsible tenure governance. Bridging gaps in women’s access to and control over land requires not only their visibility in cadastral databases, but also their active inclusion throughout all stages of cadastral processes, consistent with the VGGT principles of equality and non‑discrimination. As cadastre is information-and information is power- advancing toward a system that recognizes women as full stakeholders is key to challenging patriarchal structures embedded in administrative procedures and social practices.

[79] Finally, the experience of Decree 0462 of 2025 demonstrates that it is possible to develop regulatory instruments through intercultural dialogue, acknowledging both the multidimensionality of Indigenous territories and their distinct normative systems. This experience reflects the VGGT emphasis on respecting legitimate tenure systems and cultural diversity. At the same time, it underscores that, while the multipurpose cadastre opens new opportunities, significant structural barriers to broad and equitable participation remain.

[80] The challenge ahead lies in consolidating a model that integrates technical innovation with social and cultural justice, advancing toward truly inclusive and responsible land governance in Colombia.
