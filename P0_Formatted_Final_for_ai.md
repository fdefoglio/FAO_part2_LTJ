[0] **The role of the VGGT in advancing land policies and reforms: An introduction**

[1] # 1. Land policy reform between momentum and challenge

[2] Land policy reform – understood as a multidimensional and iterative process encompassing the formulation, revision, and implementation of legal, regulatory, and institutional frameworks governing access to, control over, and use of land – is increasingly reemerging not only as a central issue in public policy debates, but as a necessary response to intensifying and often conflicting pressures on land systems worldwide. Growing competition over land, driven by climate change, largescale landbased investments, food system transformation, rapid urbanization, and the expansion of energy infrastructure, is generating complex tradeoffs and contestation among multiple land users and stakeholders. These dynamics are heightening risks of land conflicts, inequality, and tenure insecurity, while exposing the limitations of existing governance frameworks.

[3] A substantial body of literature highlights that these pressures are not only increasing in intensity but also becoming more evidently interconnected. Processes such as agricultural commercialization, extractive industry projects, socioeconomic transformations and urban expansion are reshaping patterns of land access and control and amplifying competing claims (Deininger *et al.*, 2011; Cotula, 2013; IPES-Food, 2024). At the same time, climate change and environmental degradation are placing additional strain on already scarce and degraded land resources, further intensifying competition and conflict (IPCC, 2019; FAO, ILC and CIRAD, 2026). These processes foster deep changes in the nature and operation of land governance systems, such as customary authorities and their relations with state systems (Chimhowu, 2019), and the terms of integration of Indigenous territories into national societies (Merino, 2021). The unfolding and outcomes of such processes tend to reflect and reinforce social inequalities, exacerbating patterns of differentiation based on wealth, income, status, gender, age and other factors (e.g. FAO, 2018, on gender).

[4] In this context, land policy reform is increasingly recognized as a critical instrument to manage competing land claims, strengthen tenure security, and promote more equitable and sustainable land-use outcomes. The adoption of the Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure of Land, Fisheries and Forests in the Context of National Food Security (VGGT) in 2012 has played a catalytic role in this regard, providing an internationally endorsed normative framework to guide land governance reforms and address overlapping and contested rights (FAO, 2012).

[5] Since their adoption, the VGGT have been ‘appropriated’ and used by states, social movements, professionalized non-governmental organizations and the private sector to address a wide range of land policy issues (Cotula, 2017; Pierri, Anseeuw and Campolina, 2025). Evidence from the 2026 Status of Land Governance and Tenure (FAO, ILC and CIRAD, 2026) indicates that a growing number of countries have undertaken land policy, legal, and institutional reforms that are at least partially aligned with VGGT principles: seventy-one countries globally have engaged in such reform processes, albeit with considerable variation in the aims, scope, depth, and implementation of the reforms (FAO, ILC and CIRAD, 2026). Empirical analysis by Essimi Biloa and Gaarde (2024) further highlights that this reform momentum has been particularly pronounced across Africa. They demonstrate that approximately thirty countries across the continent have undertaken land policy reforms, many of which have been guided by the VGGT, often alongside the African Union’s Framework and Guidelines on Land Policy in Africa. These reforms have contributed to important advances, including greater recognition of customary tenure systems, the strengthening of women’s land rights, and the revision of legal and institutional frameworks governing land administration.

[6] These findings are consistent with broader comparative analyses, which also show, however, that while progress in policy and legislative development has been significant, implementation is uneven and shaped by institutional and political economy constraints. Such constraints have substantially limited the extent to which policy commitments have resulted in effective tenure security outcomes (International Land Coalition and Welthungerhilfe, 2023; Wegerif, Coulibaly and Ouedraogo, 2025).

[7] Renewed momentum for advancing land policy reform was most recently underscored by the International Conference on Agrarian Reform and Rural Development (ICARRD+20), convened in Colombia in February 2026. Building on the legacy of the original ICARRD held by FAO in Brazil in 2006, the conference marked a significant moment of political reengagement with land and agrarian policy agendas at the international level. It reaffirmed a comprehensive understanding of agrarian change that situates land access and tenure security within broader national and global political economy, ecologies and governance frameworks and initiated a follow-up process, including consideration of convening a new ICARRD in the coming years.

[8] Since the adoption of the VGGT in 2012, numerous development partners have provided technical and other assistance to support processes for reforming land policies and practices in wide-ranging contexts. Such assistance engaged efforts from across the policy cycle from diagnostics and policy formulation to legal reform, institutional strengthening, and implementation. Some of these efforts have secured concrete steps forwards in both policy and practice, typically through bringing together complementary resources, skillsets, insights and networks, and over time developing evidence on concrete approaches that can deliver change in politically sensitive policy arenas. Support initiatives have also linked reform processes to the monitoring of the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly indicators related to secure tenure rights and gender equality in land ownership, reinforcing land governance as a measurable development outcome.

[9] Taken together, these developments signal a renewed international commitment to land policy reform as a cornerstone of inclusive, resilient, and sustainable rural transformation. They also point to a substantial and growing body of experience with supporting land policy reform in practice, which can provide important insights into how to design and implement initiatives that can translate VGGT principles into practical outcomes.

[10] # 2. The special issue

[11] This special issue examines lessons from practical experience with aligning land policies and reforms with the VGGT. Discussing country case studies that range from Latin America to Africa and Southeast Asia, the special issue brings together reflective pieces from scholar-practitioners who have first-hand knowledge of (and often direct involvement in) the policy processes they place under examination.

[12] From a methodological standpoint, this emphasis on practitioner insights sustains a distinctive form of knowledge that can complement social research methods centered on quantitative or qualitative data collection and analysis. The approach highlights questions of positionality, in that it calls on scholar-practitioners to take a step back from their daily work and to produce detached reflections on processes that are close to their professional lives. At the same time, it can offer unique insights into the complex realities of policy making, illuminating aspects that may be inaccessible to external researchers.

[13] The result is a collection of articles that provides insights into wide-ranging aspects of land policy reform, cutting across diverse contexts, land problems, reform objectives, implementation approaches, support initiatives and practical outcomes. The emphasis is on the practical and the applied: the articles reflect not only on crystallizing policy commitments and/or giving effect to them through legislative or institutional reforms; but also on translating policy into real change, such as through land registration systems or local consultation processes. Conversely, several contributions reflect on initiatives to ground national policy reform into local-level experiences.

[14] Collectively, the articles shed light on the long-term historical trajectories that need to be understood in order to make sense of, and support, land policy reform; on the diverse and complementary roles of different actors in reform processes, both within and beyond the state; on the complex interplay of political and technical dimensions that affect both challenges and solutions; on navigating pluralistic systems of governance, law, religion and economy, with land connecting multiple ideologies, understandings, and logics for authority; and on what approaches work, and what conditions need to be in place, for advancing effective reforms aligned with the VGGT.

[15] # 3. A few Thematic highlights

[16] Overall, the articles in the collection highlight the continued relevance of the VGGT in land policy reform processes. The articles document how, since their adoption, the VGGT have been resorted to and used in different ways, in diverse policy contexts, from national legislation and policy reform to community by-laws and commissions of enquiry. Besides providing an authoritative reference point for addressing specific land policy issues, the VGGT have also been mobilized and wielded to catalyze social and institutional processes and build momentum for change in the first place, providing what has been described in the Uganda case study presented by Ochom as a ‘shared global policy ethos’.

[17] At the same time, the case studies highlight the complex relation that can exist between normative guidance and policy change, with important reforms being primarily grounded in national political compromise, and with the VGGT offering an anchor for supporting implementation. These circumstances point to the role of the VGGT not just as a text that offers specific guidance on tenure issues but as a living instrument that can be and has been adapted to diverse and evolving socio-political contexts. The importance of understanding the operation of the VGGT in specific socio-political contexts is discussed in many of the special issue articles, which also note the interface between the VGGT and regional instruments such as the African Union Guiding Principles on Large-Scale Land-Based Investments, as well as the Framework and Guidelines on Land Policy, highlighting the interconnections between regional and global soft-law instruments as they are wielded in national settings.

[18] The case studies indicate acute awareness of the complex political economies and power asymmetries that characterize land relations, and thus inevitably land policy reform. They also illustrate how this awareness can be translated into sophisticated multi-actor and multi-level engagement strategies that harness technically viable solutions, leverage moments of heightened political momentum for change, and ultimately secure policy breakthroughs that technical insight alone would have struggled to deliver. Seufert and Kone illustrate these aspects in their discussion of the adoption of the Loi Foncière Agricole in Mali, and of subsequent initiatives to support its implementation. Similarly, Gamboa reflects on experiences with a social planning metamodel in Guatemala that connects knowledge and action through shared learning, and balancing of ethical, technical and political dimensions, to translate political commitment into 'technical capacities and operational tools'.

[19] At the same time, the case studies point to the continued challenges of translating political economy insights into approaches that can secure concrete change in the face of vested interests or policy inertia, or that can protect those changes during periods of institutional volatility. They also point to persistent implementation gaps and to the deep challenges that continue to constrain impact at scale. Fragmentation between state-based and customary systems, erosion of accountability in customary tenure systems, weak cross-sectoral integration, and limited alignment between national frameworks and local realities continue to undermine effectiveness and inclusiveness of land governance as experienced by millions of rural people (Knight, 2012; Osei-Tutu, Brobbey and Agyei, 2021). The costs of implementation, tensions generated through perceived purposes of formal land recognition and registration, and the risks associated with patchwork external funding also emerged as common concerns for establishing social legitimacy and rolling out national land programmes.

[20] The central challenge moving forward, then, lies not in the articulation of commitments, but in their effective translation into tangible changes in land relations on the ground. Evidence from VGGT implementation shows that advances in policy and legal reform do not automatically translate into improved tenure security or reduced conflict. Instead, outcomes are shaped by institutional capacities, political economy dynamics, power asymmetries and the quality of coordination across governance levels (Hall *et al.*, 2016; FAO, 2023; Wegerif *et al.*, 2025). In this regard, Codispoti, Gaarde and Malloum, in their article on Chad, illustrate that the outcomes of land reforms are determined not only by the technical content of the reforms themselves, but also by stakeholders’ ability to navigate institutional constraints, reconcile divergent interests, address power imbalances, and maintain effective coordination across multiple levels of governance and stakeholder engagement throughout the various stages of the reform process**.**

[21] Therefore, advancing the next generation of land policy reform requires a stronger focus on how reforms are implemented in practice, including in relation to their institutional design, sequencing, and coordination across scales. In this regard, the case studies share several approaches that are specifically oriented towards translating policy reforms into real change. Reflecting on experience from Tanzania, Massay discusses approaches for strengthening women’s land rights through increased awareness of protections, VGGT-inspired civil society advocacy, and sustained governance engagement that advances gender-sensitive regulation and practice at the local level. The articles by Santofimio** **on Colombia and by Wabwire and Burke on Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania discuss progress toward implementing community-driven land surveys and cadasters, which enhance social legitimacy, deliver faster, cheaper and more scalable boundary determinations, and hold the promise of interoperable land governance systems.

[22] Further, the case studies emphasize the overarching importance of grounding recognition in inclusive, flexible and pragmatic governance mechanisms capable of sustaining themselves, including the article by Essimi Biloa and Tshimanga on the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The article by Schultz and Jenni on experience in Laos highlights the importance of consent-seeking in land registration programming.

[23] Most of the articles highlight contexts of growing land pressure linked to population growth, economic development, climate change, and the energy transition. They also highlight increasingly complex and intersecting challenges that are being brought into focus through land. These are multi-sectoral (such as agriculture, mining and other sectors), multi-level (such as local, national and international), multi-dimensional (such as laws, economies and institutions), and multi-values (such as identity, culture, and histories), all of which need to be carefully reconciled through effective land governance (Croese *et al.*, 2021).

[24] These trends point to the continued relevance of implementing the VGGT – and to the need for knowledge and learning processes that can bridge research and practice, feeding insights from practical experiences into reform design and implementation. The articles in this special issue are a contribution to this agenda, offering insights into how the VGGT can best be operationalized to support inclusive and transformative land policy reforms.
