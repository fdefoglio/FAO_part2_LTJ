[0] # Role of International and Regional Land Tenure Guidelines in Improving Women’s Participation in Land Decision Making Bodies. Experience from Tanzania

[1] # Abstract

[2] In the recent decades, there has been significant increase in the number of international instruments as well as municipal legislation which pushes for women’s participation in land administration. There has also been increase in a number of global and continental land tenure guides which have shaped and influenced national and local level policy and programmatic interventions which seek to increase women’ participation in land administration.  This paper seeks to highlight international and regional legal instruments and guides which guarantee women’s participation in land administration and how this has been realised in practice in Tanzania. International legal instruments such as the Universal Declarations on Human Rights and the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women as well as guides such as the Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure of Land, Fisheries and Forests in the Context of National Food Security (VGGT) and the Framework and Guidelines on Land Policy in Africa (F&G) will be discussed. The paper also aims at showing the extent to which Tanzanian laws have safeguarded women’s participation in land governance. Moreover, the paper shows some of the gray areas in the laws and the emerging renovations, mostly advocated by Civil Society Organizations in Tanzania, to address such gaps. It argues that, although Tanzania has progressive land laws, there are still some unfinished business in the laws and offers some recommendations for policy considerations. Similarly, it urges that the newly adopted National Land Policy in Tanzania has strong provisions which safeguard women’s participation in decision making bodies and taken aspirations of the VGGT and F&G

[3] ## 1. Introduction

[4] Land reforms in sub-Saharan Africa in the 1990s were characterized by a shift from earlier state-centric policies to a more local modes of land governance through decentralization of land institutions (Toulmin, 2000; Boone, 2007; Deininger, 2003; Lipton, 2009), recognition of the need to protect the rights of women through various gender-specific regulations (Wily, 2003; Whitehead and Tsikata, 2003) and the recognition of existing rights including customary rights (Knight, 2010; Pedersen, 2013). Even with progressive provisions on women’s land rights, land reforms have been frequently criticized for disadvantaging women and for being gendered (Berry, 1993; Jacobs, 2010; Jackson, 2003; Nyamu-Musembi, 2008). Some scholars have, however, demonstrated ways in which land reforms have enhanced gender equity and secured women’s land rights (Agarwal, 2003; Boone, 2007, Varley, 2007, Whitehead and Tsikata, 2003).

[5] Tanzania’s 1995 National Land Policy and 1999 Land Laws are considered among the most gender-aware in sub-Saharan Africa (Tsikata, 2003; Ikdahl, et al, 2005; Knight, 2010). The policy and the laws are hailed for recognizing equal land rights between men and women, inclusion of women in land governance bodies, recognition of individual and joint land ownership, prohibition of all forms of discrimination, and the protection of vulnerable groups (Knight, 2010; Rwegasira, 2012; Magawa & Hansungule, 2018).

[6] Implementation of progressive land reforms in sub-Saharan Africa generally and in Tanzania specifically, has been slow, uneven and donor funded project based (Pedersen, 2015). The challenges in the land sector such as the land pressure, commercialization, and the recent global land rush—which has caused loss of biodiversity and limits women’s access to common resources such as edible wild plants, water and firewood has brought to the surface gray areas in the reform process which need further reviews (Rossi and Lambrou 2008, Haywood et al 2008, Daley 2011, Daley and Pallas 2014). One such area is participation of women in land decision making bodies.

[7] Daley and Pallas (2014) identified four major vulnerability dimensions for women in the wave of increased land pressure caused by the global land rush: productive resources, relative income poverty, participation in decision making, and physical vulnerability. Participation in decision making was also discussed by Wandie (2009) and Nhatumbo & Salomuo (2010), who have argued that women do not usually participate in the negotiations held in their communities about potential land deals.

[8] In an effort to address the challenges related to the global land rush such as those on women’s participation in land administration decision making bodies, at the international level, the Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure of Land, Fisheries and Forests in the Context of National Food Security was endorsed by governments and other stakeholder groups after years of consultations in different regions through the Committee on World Food Security (CFS) in 2012. At the African regional level, the African Union adopted the Framework and Guidelines on Land Policy in Africa (F&G) in 2010 and the Guiding Principles on Large Scale Land Based Investments in Africa in 2014. Other guidelines regulating land-based investments and land policies have been developed at the global and regional levels.

[9] These guidelines have been used to mobilize social movements at the international, regional and country levels to demand, among other things, secure land rights for women. International Land Coalition and La Via Campensina are two prominent organizations that have mobilized movements of civil society organizations and farmer organizations to demand, among other things, secure land rights. At the pan African level, rural women across the continent mobilized themselves and developed their charter of demands in 2016. The Food and Agricultural Organization of the United Nations (FAO) FAO has played a leading role in supporting the uptake of VGGT at country and (sub)regional level. FAO provides technical assistance and capacitybuilding support to governments and nonstate actors, including civil society, to help them apply the VGGT principles in policy processes, legal reforms, and implementation practices.

[10] This paper seeks to explore two local level innovations that have been influenced by the regional and international voluntary land tenure guidelines and which in turn enhance women’s participation in land governance decision making bodies. The first innovation is on village level bylaws that have been developed to address the silence in the principal legislation regarding the women’s quota in the main village level land governing body. The bylaws are inspired by the international and regional land tenure guidelines and informed by the negative impact of large-scale land-based investment that have not fully consulted women at its inception stages. The case study demonstrates ways in which regional and international voluntary land tenure guidelines can be domesticated through a locally binding municipal law. The second innovation case relates to trained local volunteers who provide training to villagers and encourage women to join, attend and meaningfully participate in land decision making bodies. These volunteers undertake human rights literacy work, a key component in the implementation of the legislation.

[11] The paper employs secondary data sources through the desk/literature review on women’s land rights, international legal instruments on the rights of women, and reports from Tanzania that are relevant to the subject matter. Similarly, the author draws from his own previous experience on the two cases. The paper is divided into six parts. The first part is the introduction, The second part looks at the international and regional developments on women’s participation in decision making and in land governance. The third part highlights the legal framework in Tanzania and the extent to which it safeguards women’s rights to participate in land decision making bodies and the gaps in the framework. The fourth part presents the two cases of municipal legislation and the role of the trained volunteers, both of which were heavily influenced by the regional and international voluntary land tenure guidelines and their role in enhancing women’s participation in land management and administration. The fifth section discusses key issues and sums up the paper. The next section focuses on the international and regional development on women participation in decision making and those related to land.

[12] # 2. International and regional development on women’s participation in decision making

[13] ## 2.1 Casting the Net wide

[14] Women’s struggles against inequalities have been spearheaded by women themselves through their organizing in subnational, national and international organizations, even before the establishment of moder-day conventional national, regional and international women’s rights bodies. In 1888 in Washington, the International Council of Women was formed uniting women not just in America but across the world to advance women’s rights including their equal representation in decision making bodies (Mwaikasu, 2015).

[15] The United Nations and its specialized agencies have, however, played a pivotal role in influencing the direction of African governments regarding women’s rights. The United Nations (UN) Charter (Article 55, 56 and 62) and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) (Article 1,2,4,6,15,17,23(1&2), 25(2) and 26) have been the springboards for the concerted effort of the world community in eradicating all forms of discrimination against women. For instance, UDHR’s articles 7 on equality before law and nondiscrimination, 17 on right to own property, and 21 on right to take part in the government have shaped the provisions of land laws - specifically- those on equal rights to access and own land and to participate in land governance bodies.

[16] Through the work of the Commission on the Status of Women and other specialized U.N Agencies such as the International Labour Organisation (ILO) and the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organisation (UNESCO), the Economic and Social Council of the UN was able to pass important resolutions on the rights of women between 1953-1955 (UN-CSW, 1958;97-99), all of which were aimed at implementing the UN Charter and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, by endeavoring to eliminate the recorded prevalent disadvantages faced by women specifically on securing equal political rights and economic opportunities for women, equal pay for equal work, and the role of technical assistance programs in safeguarding the rights of women.

[17] Other UN instruments having an important bearing on the status of women are the supplementary Convention on the Abolition of Slavery, the Slave Trade and the Institutions and Practices Similar to Slavery of 1956, and the Convention on the Political Rights of Women of 1952. These formed the basis of subsequent UN discussions on the same issues in 1965 in Tehran, and in 1966 in Geneva, culminating in a Draft Declaration on the Elimination of Discrimination Against Women which was later unanimously adopted by the UN General Assembly (Resolution 2263(XXII) 7 Nov 1967). The Declaration provided the basis for the UN Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women of 1979 (Resolution 34/180 of 18th December 1979) which requires States to ensure women in rural areas are granted equal treatment in land and agrarian reform, guarantees women equal legal capacity to own and administer property, equal voice in civic life, policy creation, and decision-making processes (Art 6,8,14 and 15 of CEDAW). Prior to these, there were two important UN General Assembly Resolutions; the General Assembly Resolution 2263 (XXII) of November 7, 1967, which required states to adopt legislative measures to ensure that women, married and unmarried, got equal rights as men in matters of civil law especially to acquire, administer, enjoy, dispose of and inherit property, including property acquired during marriage. General Assembly Resolution 2542 (XXIV) of December 11, 1969required the establishment of forms of land ownership and of means of production, which precluded any kind of exploitation of man, ensure equal right to property for all and create conditions leading to genuine equality among people in order to attain true social progress (Article 2 of UNGA Declaration on Social Progress and Development).The declaration of the UN on “UN Decade of Women (1976-1985)” gave further global impetus to the protection of rights of women.

[18] Apart from the UN Conventions referred to above, other notable international political events organized by women to build the political will within the international community to enhance the status of women were the international women’s conferences held in Mexico City in 1975; in Copenhagen, Denmark in 1980; in Nairobi, Kenya in 1985; and the Beijing conference of 1995, which came up with the “Beijing Declaration”. The Declaration touched on various issues relating to women’s rights, formulated as a demand by women to be accorded the same status and the rights as men. Importantly, the Declaration provides explicit determination to ensure women’s equal access to economic resources, including land and a commitment to equal rights, equal responsibilities and equal opportunities and to the equal participation of women and men in all national, regional and international bodies and policy-making processes (Declaration 35&36).

[19] In September 1997 the Southern African Development Community (SADC) member states, including Tanzania, made a “Declaration” in which Heads of State committed to, inter alia; enhancing equal representation of women and men in decision making of member states (Declaration I), and promoting women’s full access to and control over productive resources such as land (Declaration II).

[20] Apart from the adoption of the African Charter on Human and Peoples Rights and Duties in 1981 by Organisation of African Union- OAU (now AU), on the 11th of July 2003, the African Union adopted a Protocol to the Charter on Human and Peoples’ Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa. Adopted in Maputo, the Protocol commonly known as the “Maputo Protocol”, and came into force on the 25th of November 2005. The Protocol emphasizes equal representation of women and men at all levels, including electoral processes, and enjoins state parties to combat and eliminate all forms of violence against women, in similar terms as found in the SADC Declaration.

[21] As a further expression of urgent concerns for the realization of the provisions of the Maputo Protocol, particularly those relating to equality between men and women and the corollary measures to eliminate all forms of discrimination and the eradication of all forms of violence against women was the “Resolution” of the East African Legislative Assembly of November 2009, adopted in Arusha, Tanzania. By this resolution, the Assembly made an urgent call upon member states particularly regarding the elimination of all forms of violence against women in the region.

[22] ## 2.2 Narrowing to land tenure and women’s participation in land decision making

[23] At the international level, the Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure of Land, Fisheries and Forests in the Context of National Food Security (VGGT), 2012 and Principles for Responsible Investment in Agriculture and Food Systems, 2014 are perhaps the most famous and comprehensive documents on land tenure and responsible investment in the last decade. VGGT strongly encompasses equality and justice principles and promotes equitable tenure rights and access to land, fisheries and forests, for all, including women and men, youth and vulnerable and traditionally marginalized people, within the national context (para 3). It places obligations upon states to remove and prohibit all forms of discrimination related to tenure rights (para 4.6), and consider the particular obstacles faced by women and girls regarding tenure and associated tenure rights. In addition, States are required to take measures to ensure that legal and policy frameworks provide adequate protections for women and that laws that recognize women’s tenure rights are implemented and enforced (para 5.4). Moreover, effective participation of all community members, men, women and youth, in decisions regarding their tenure systems should be promoted through their local or traditional institutions, including in the case of collective tenure systems (para 9.2).Furthermore, the State are encouraged to set up multi-stakeholder platforms and frameworks at local, national and regional levels or use such existing platforms and frameworks to collaborate on the implementation of VGGTs (para 26.2). This process should be inclusive, participatory, gender sensitive, implementable, cost effective and sustainable (Ibid.). Multi-stakeholder platforms on land exist in Tanzania and are convened regularly.

[24] Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) is another global instrument which has helped elevate women’s land rights. The theme of the paper fits under Goal Five (achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls) of the Sustainable Development Goals, 2030. Each SDG Goal is made of targets, and each target has indicators which are used to monitor and measure the implementation progress. Goal 5, Target 5.A is particularly relevant to the paper and states “Undertake reforms to give women equal rights to economic resources, as well as access to ownership and control over land and other forms of property, financial services, inheritance and natural resources, in accordance with national laws”. Indicator 5.a.2 monitors reforms in the legal and policy framework that support women’s equal rights to economic resources, ownership and control over land, with a focus on the adoption of legal reforms to promote women’s land rights. Six proxies have been approved to collect information under the indicator. One is on women’s participation in land management and administration institutions and states “does the legal and policy framework mandate women’s participation in land management and administration institutions?” (Proxy F). What this SDG indictor have done is to enshrine “women’s participation in the land management and administration institutions” as an important issue in assessing laws, in each country globally, and the extent to which they protect women’s rights and ultimately realize the goal of gender equality.

[25] At the Africa regional level, there is the Declaration on Land Issues and Challenges in Africa, 2009 in which AU member States resolved to “strengthen security of land tenure for women which require special attention.” In addition, the Framework and Guidelines on Land Policy in Africa, 2010 promotes strengthening the land rights of women through a variety of mechanisms including the enactment of legislation that allows women to enforce documented claims to land within and outside marriage (see para 3.1.4). This should come hand in hand with equal rights for women to inherit and bequeath land, co-ownership of registered land by spouses and the promotion of women’s participation in land administration structures (Ibid).

[26] The Framework and Guidelines on Land Policy in Africa has put in place factors that should inform comprehensive land policy development including that “deliberate steps must be taken to ensure full and informed participation of women - Africa’s primary land users – in policy development and implementation (para 4.1.1)”. Other areas covered by the Framework and Guidelines include; recognizing the role of indigenous institutions to allow sufficient women’s representations (para 4.5.2), continuous public engagement through decentralized structures that ensure participation of women (para 5.3.5), and stakeholder agreement on what should be tracked which must include equality of access to decision-making by all stakeholders, particularly women (para 6.2.2).

[27] The Guiding Principles on Large Scale Land Based Investments in Africa, 2014, is another regional document developed by the African Union on land tenure and land-based investment in Africa. The document consists of six fundamental principles that seek to ensure that large scale land-based investment (LSLBI) respect the land rights of women, recognize their voice, generate meaningful opportunities for women alongside men, and do not exacerbate the marginalization of women (principle 4). Its entire chapter five is dedicated to the rights and benefits of women with three principles that are focused on women. The principles state that LSLBI must observe and respect women’s equal rights to own, access, control, and use land, as the case may be, under different tenure regimes (principle 12) and contribute to sustainable development through gender sensitive employment creation and broad-based wealth creation which benefits women (principle 13). It further seeks to promote gender equality in land governance in national laws as a prerequisite to ensuring that LSLBI promote sustainable development (principle 11).

[28] In October 2015, the AU Special Technical Committee on agriculture, water and environment recommended that Member States allocate at least 30% of land to women; improve land rights of women through legislative/other mechanisms, in order to give practical effect to the AU declaration on Land through which all African states committed to ensuring equitable access to land for all land users and strengthen women’s land rights. A year later, rural women across the continent mobilized in an iconic movement to the foot of Mount Kilimanjaro with their charter containing fifteen demands on land rights. Relevant to the theme of this paper is their demand for “50% participation of women in decision-making bodies and implementation of land issues and matters (including in the valuation of land and payment of compensation for natural resources) so that they can speak and defend their land rights (Kilimanjaro Initiative, 2016).” These demands were later endorsed by the African Union.

[29] Having discussed the international developments on women’s participation in decision making and the extent to which land tenure has been addressed, the next section delves into the Tanzanian legal framework governing land and the way in which it has addressed women’s right to participate in decision making bodies.

[31] ## The National Legal Framework

[32] Customary and statutory land governance institutions tend to have more men than women because land governance is traditionally viewed as domain of men (Giovarelli,2009; Agarwal 2003; Walker 2003, Whitehead and Tsikata, 2003) and this contributes to the weakening of women’s land rights (Giovarelli, 2009; Agarwal, 2003; Walker, 2003; Whitehead and Tsikata, 2003). In order to address the gender imbalances in decision making bodies, Tanzania has taken various steps to increase the number of women representatives in decision-making bodies (including those related to land). The steps taken by the government is to establish institutional framework governing land from the national to the village level, put a specific quota of women representation in decision making bodies, and to strengthen such institutions through land rights literacy indicating their composition and mandates. This section highlights laws in Tanzania that have established land governance bodies and specific quota of women represented in such bodies.

[33] When Tanganyika (later Tanzania) gained her independence in 1961, not much was changed from the inherited colonial Land Ordinance of 1923, except the authority in which land is vested. The legal framework that governed land replaced the governor as the custodian of the public land with the president and there were no gender consideration in the management and administration of land (Hakiardhi, 2005; Rwegasira, 2012). With the coming of neo-liberal policies between 1985 and early 1990s, the Government of Tanzania reformed its policies to embrace market economy and privatization in the 1990s (Shivji, 1998; Tsikata, 2003; Mbilinyi, 1988). The neo-liberal policies embodied equality provisions, giving men and women equal access to land and representation in its governance (Knight, 2010). Its implementations, however, are criticized for being gendered, frequently exacerbated conflicts and patterns of unequal access to land based on gender, age, ethnicity and class (Peters, 2004).  In early 1990s, the Presidential Commission of Inquiry on Land Matters, also known as the Shivji Commission, was formed to investigate land conflicts all over the country. The commission’s report led to the formulation of the 1995 National Land Policy and the 1999 Land Acts, namely the Land Act number 4 and the Village Land Act number 5 of 1999 which have embraced equality provisions and quota of women in land governance structures as shown below.

[34] The first piece of legislation is the Land Act, Act number 4 of 1999- [CAP 113 RE. 2023] which provide for the basic law in relation to land other than the village land, the management of land and related matters. Under S. 17 (1) established the National Land Advisory Council with mandate to review and advise the government on policies, legislation, and procedures related to land use and administration. The Act states “there is hereby established a National Land Advisory Council which shall have not less than seven members and not more than eleven members appointed by the Minister and the Chairman of the council shall be appointed by the President”. Section 17 (2) states “in appointing members to the Council, the Minister shall have regard to the importance of ensuring a fair balance of men and women in the Council”. The Land (Composition and Procedures of the National Land Advisory Council) Regulations, 2001. Regulation 3 provides that quota and states “the Council shall consist of seven members three of whom shall be women as follows: 3(g) one representative from the Ministry Responsible for Gender. 3(h) one-woman lawyers representing an Association of lawyers”.

[35] The second legislation is the Village Land Act, Act number 5 of 1999 [CAP 114 RE 2023] which provide for the management and administration of land in villages which is composed of 70 percent of land mass in Tanzania and for other related matters. This law establishes the village Adjudication Committee under Section 53 (1) and its composition under Section 53(2). The committee has functions to determine boundaries of and interest in land which is subject to village adjudication. The Act states that “A Village Adjudication Committee shall consist of not less than six nor more than nine persons, of whom not less than three persons shall be women, who shall serve for a term of three years. Moreover, Section 60 (1) establishes the Village Land Council -a body responsible for dispute resolution. It states that “for the purposes of this Part, every village shall establish a Village Land Council to mediate between and assist parties to arrive at a mutually acceptable solution on any matter concerning village land. Section 60: (2) provides for the composition of the Village Land Council and states that “where a village council establishes a Village Land Council, that council shall consist of not less than five nor more than seven persons, of which not less than two shall be women, who shall be– (a) nominated by the village council; and (b) approved by the village assembly. The quorum of the meeting is covered under Section 60: (9) which states that “the quorum of a meeting of the village land council shall be four persons, of which at least one shall be a woman”.

[36] The management of the village land is vested in the Village Council (s. 8(1) VLA) -the elected body composed of 25 members (s. 56(1) of Local Government Act, 1982).The Act states that the village council shall exercise the functions of management in accordance with the principles applicable to a trustee managing property on behalf of a beneficiary as if the council were a trustee of, and the villagers and other persons resident in the village were beneficiaries under, a trust of the village land (s.8(1) VLA).A village council shall not allocate land or grant a customary right of occupancy without a prior approval of the Village Assembly (s. 8(5) VLA). The Village Assembly is composed of all adult villagers 18 years of age and above (s. 55 LGA). Thus, the Village Assembly is the supreme body that provides approval and decisions regarding the village land. However, the law does not have the quota required for women in the Village Assembly Meeting. This is a lacuna which is discussed at length in the next section.

[37] The third legislation is the Courts (Land Disputes Settlements) Act, Act number 2 of 2002 which provide for the establishment of land dispute settlement machinery and related matters. It establishes the Ward Tribunal under section 10. When this tribunal was formed to entertain land cases in 2002, it had all functional powers of the court including making ruling. However, the amendments made in 2021 have only retained powers of the tribunal to mediate land cases. On its composition, Section 11 states “each tribunal shall consist of not less than four nor more than eight members of whom three shall be women who shall be elected by Ward Committee as provided for under section 4 of Ward Tribunal Act, 1985”. Section14 (1) provides for the quorum and states that “the Tribunal shall in all matters of mediation consist of three members at least one of whom shall be a woman”.

[38] The fourth legislation is the Land Use Planning Act, Act number 10 of 2007 which provide for procedures for the preparation, administration and enforcement of land use plans at the village, district, regional or landscape level. The Act establishes the National Land Use Planning Commission under section 6 and stipulates its functions under section 7. The commission provides assistance to all land use planning authorities, prepares land use plans, and monitors their implementations. Similarly, it coordinates all sectors on collective standards, stimulates public-private partnerships on land use plans, undertake education programs, documentation and dissemination work. Section 6(1) provides for gendered composition and state that “there is hereby established a Commission to be known as the National Land use Planning Commission which shall consist of- (2) In appointing the members of the Commission under subsection (1) (b), the Minister shall ensure that at least three of the members are women.”

[39] As shown above, the Government made a deliberate move to increase women’s participation in decision-making institutions at local and national levels land governing bodies by including gender quota in the land legal regime. The move was accompanied by the amendment of the Local Government (Urban Authorities) Act of 1982. The amendments were made in 2000 and established affirmative action to mandate the participation of women in local government decision-making bodies. Just like in the Village Land Act, the law regulating the establishment and management of villages, districts and townships requires women to constitute one-third of the members of each District Council and one-fourth of the members of each township authority and village council. For clarity, the local government governance structure in Tanzania is decentralised into two arms, namely, the urban and rural authorities. The urban authorities are made up of city, municipal, town, division, ward, and mtaa (sub-ward) councils. And, the rural authorities are made up of district, town authorities working in rural areas, the village and sub-village council.

[40] It would be good to end this part by highlighting the changes in the National Land Policy of 1995. The Government of Tanzania started the process of reviewing its 1995 National Land Policy in 2016. After a long policy consultative and review process involving multi-stakeholder platforms on land, the 2023 revision of the 1995 National Land Policy was officially Launched in March 2025 (Massay 2025). Multistakeholder Platforms led by Landesa, Tanzania Land Alliance, Hakiardhi and Care International provided joint policy recommendations and convened policy dialogues with policy drafters in 2018 and 2019. Moreover, they convened a National Land Forum involving government officials, research institutions, journalists, CSOs and community members to discuss the national land policy to validate policy recommendations in 2019. The Revised edition of the policy, which was launched in 2025, has strong provision on women’s participation in decision making bodies as it promotes balance representation of men and women in land governance and administration (para 3.10.2 (iv)). Moreover, the policy requires the Government to put in place mechanism to ensure equal gender rights in access of land rights (para 3.10.2.(i)), and guarantee the women have equal opportunity to access land rights (para 3.10.2.(ii) ). In addition, the policy ensures public awareness on gender equality in land rights and land use (para 3.10.2. (iii)). All these provisions have been adopted due to progressive regional and international tenure guidelines such as the VGGTs and F&G discussed in part two above. The next part will address the practice in the implantation of land laws and its implication on women’s participation in decision-making bodies using the two innovation cases.

[41] ## Gap between in the Law and Implementation

[42] ## Improving Women’s Participation through Village Bylaws

[43] In 2015, the World Resource Institute (WRI)-a global environmental organization based in the United States of America, Tanzania Women Lawyers Association (TAWLA)-a professional organization of women lawyers in Tanzania and Lawyers’ Environmental Action Team (LEAT)- a premier organization working on environmental rights in Tanzania- conducted a scoping study in Kidugalo and Vilabwa villages in Kisarawe District, one of the districts in the Coast Region near Dar es Salaam in Tanzania with ongoing agribusiness investments. The study found limits on women’s participation in consultation processes linked to the investments. The research identified some of the root causes of women’s limited participation, including high levels of illiteracy amongst women, the timing of Village Assembly and Village Council meetings, the burden of domestic chores, including cooking and fetching water, and the weight of local customary practices restricting the ability of women to speak out (Kisambu 2016). The project also reviewed the regulatory framework, highlighting the entry points that national law offers to promote gender equality in land relations and implementation gaps. One of the challenges identified was lack of law enforcement. The provisions in the land laws that promote women’s rights are not implemented by the institutions mandated to manage and administer land. A strong recommendation was therefore made to develop gender-sensitive village by-laws to address the gaps in village-level governance bodies.

[44]  Instead of just allowing the Village Council to draft the bylaws, TAWLA, LEAT and WRI decided to provide the strategic guidance to the Council in order to make sure that the bylaws wee gender sensitive. Developing a best practice case study was chosen as a strategic approach for influencing a more gender sensitive law-making process at the national level. It is also a less time-consuming approach, which can have a direct impact on the community as opposed to the national level advocacy for law reform. Thus, this is a bottom-up way of using the municipal bodies responsible for drafting legislations to fill the lacuna in the national laws (Kisambu, 2016).

[45] Before developing the bylaws, key gender sensitive principles were drafted as a foundation of the bylaws to be developed by organizations. Consultations with women’s groups, village leaders, community paralegals, researchers and district council officials were then conducted, sensitizing the community on the key principles and gathering their views on the same. A District Council is required under the law to approve or reject village bylaws so consulting provided them with an opportunity to strengthen their ‘buy-in’ as well as an opportunity to share research findings, policy gaps, and the key principles.

[46] The drafting of bylaws was done by the district level taskforce constituted by the legal officer, gender officer, community development officer, and the village chairpersons of the two villages selected during the research stage. When the draft bylaws were in place, more consultations with village councils, women’s groups, youth, men, and paralegals were conducted to gather inputs and to validate the bylaws. At this stage, a proposal for the person holding the village chairperson seat to rotate by gender was criticized as it was argued that it conflicted with the local government laws and was something to be determined by the prevailing political environment in the village. Afterwards, the taskforce prepared the final bylaws accommodating the views of the consulted stakeholders.

[47] The model bylaws were then submitted to the Village Councils for further discussion and approval, and a few changes were made. The Village Council submitted the bylaws to the Village Assembly, which is the final approving authority at the village level. The Village Assembly approved the bylaws without any reservations. The minutes of the Village Assembly were then submitted to the District Council for approval and registration.

[48] So far, two villages piloted under the project have approved and adopted by laws. The efforts are ongoing to get the District Council to issue a circular that can make the bylaws operational in all 64 villages of Kisarawe District Council. The same organizations that have pioneered the project have developed draft national level ‘model’ bylaws, which are now being discussed by stakeholders before submission to the responsible Minister for further administrative action. This is a major step toward scaling up this innovation throughout the country.

[49] TAWLA has been supporting weekly informal discussions with women groups who meet every Saturday in the two villages on the importance of their participation in the decision-making processes. In almost every village women have their self-help groups where they discuss different issues that affect them and collectively pool savings and decide on ways to help each other. It is expected that through these meetings women’s demand for greater participation in land governance will increase.

[50] Two more villages adopted the bylaws in September 2016 making a total of 4 villages. As of 2022, 66 villages in Kisarawe have adopted the bylaws and a nation level guide developed (TAWLA, 2022). Other than the adoption of the bylaws and advocacy at the national level, the other outcome is improved accountability and more responsive governance. These outcomes are attributed to the new knowledge and understanding among the community members who are now questioning the duty bearers on issues related to administration and management of village land.

[51] Although one of the reasons for low turn-out and inadequate participation of women in village meetings is timing (i.e. when the meetings are convened), the bylaws could not specify a convenient time for consultations and meetings on land allocation and overall land governance decisions. Moreover, the bylaws did not establish a requirement for a gender rotational system of leadership at the village level. All these were considered practically impossible to enforce as they depend on many other factors and might not conform to the spirit of the local government laws. These critical arenas for women’s participation on land governance require additional interventions to build awareness and increase women’s participation (Massay, 2019).

[52] ## 4.2 Improving women participation through Land Rights Monitors

[53] Land Rights Monitors (LRMs) are volunteers democratically elected by members of their community. The development of the LRM position was part of a communitywide capacity-building exercise supporting communities on a wide range of issues related to land governance and conflict. The role of LRMs ranges from carrying out community training on land rights and governance, to advising villagers and members of village-level land administration institutions on matters pertaining to land rights and administration. Such issues typically include land acquisition procedures, land use planning, contract negotiation with investors, and dispute settlement procedures (Massay 2016).

[54] LRMs were first supported by Hakiardhi, a Tanzanian civil society organization working on strengthening land rights, in 2008, and then by the Tanzania Natural Resources Forum (TNRF), a Tanzanian civil society organization working on natural resources rights, since 2015. At the end of the training period, community members gather to vote for two LRMs, one man and one woman. Electing one man and one woman helps promote gender equality and makes it easier for LRMs to reach both men and women in their work. As of 2017, more than 700 LRMs were working in more than 350 villages in Tanzania where Hakiardhi had its projects. TNRF has supported 28 LRMs in 14 villages where its land rights and governance capacity building programs are running. Although the work of LRMs is diverse and there are many documented impact assessments of their work, this summary will highlight their work to promote women’s land rights.

[55] To illustrate the impact of LRMs the paper looks at Kilombero District in Morogoro region in the Eastern part of Tanzania and west of Dar es Salaam where one-woman LRM has been helping women whose matrimonial property rights were denied by their husbands, or their husbands’ relatives, after divorce or the husband’s death. In one case, a woman was on the verge of being evicted from her matrimonial home by her in-laws following the death of her husband. The LRM intervened after the woman sought her advice. The LRM called both parties and explained the legal provisions that underpin women’s rights, as detailed in the Hakiardhi training manual. During the discussion, she highlighted appropriate legal remedies that would allow the woman to access her rights and pointed out the consequences that the in-laws could face should the matter reach the courts. She stressed that judicial procedures were costly and time-consuming, and suggested throughout the discussion that the matter be resolved amicably. After several consultations, both parties agreed to resolve the matter without going to court. The LRM involved village leaders and facilitated the preparation of a written settlement agreement. This successful case raised her profile in her village, and she has since been asked to provide assistance in many other cases.

[56] Women in Tanzania generally participate less actively than men in decision-making fora such as Village Assembly meetings, despite laws that guarantee them equal rights to participate. In practice, these bodies are dominated by men, and often make decisions that negatively and disproportionately affect women’s land rights. Not many rural women have time to attend such meetings because of their household and family responsibilities. Often, they take place in the evening, which makes it difficult for women to attend because of their family duties. Even when they attend, women are rarely given an opportunity to make meaningful contributions (Massay, 2019).

[57] To address these problems, women LRMs have encouraged women to attend Village Assembly meetings and to actively participate in other decision-making bodies, such as the Village Councils and the Village Land Councils. As a result, women in villages where LRMs are working effectively have been more forthcoming in engaging in decision-making processes.

[58] In Kisarawe and Kilombero districts for example, women LRMs have successfully lobbied Village Councils to schedule meetings between 2 and 4pm. They have also asked Village Councils to share the agenda of Village Assembly meetings seven days before a meeting is to take place, as required by law. When the agenda is shared in advance, women LRMs are able to discuss it with women’s groups beforehand and work out how best to air their views during the meeting. LRMs have also successfully lobbied Village Councils to allocate time for women to give their opinions on each agenda item during Village Assembly meetings. In Kilombero District, LRMs have helped women benefit from land titling and registration processes by applying for titles by themselves or jointly with their spouses.

[59] In Hanang district located in Manyara region in the northern Tanzania, a woman LRM was part of a women’s movement that reclaimed the “Sinyarayda Plain”, a grazing land that was grabbed from villages by local investors seeking to establish farms. The Plain is used by five villages for grazing but was first encroached on by farmers in 2012. Despite several village meetings and interventions from district officials from 2012 to 2014, which included establishing a resolution to stop farmers from using the grazing land, farmers continued to use the land. In March 2015, all women villagers from the five villages gathered and made a decision to stop this encroachment and loss of grazing land. They agreed to slash down all crops in the farms for three consecutive days. Women were singing “sinyarayda our land, you provided us with milk and food, you fed our cattle and made them healthy, you can’t be taken away from us” as they slashed down the crops. Police intervened with support from the investors and filed charges against the women in the High Court of Tanzania. The LRM reported the matter to Hakiardhi and sought their assistance in court. The women were represented by three lawyers in court but the lawyers for the investors decided to settle the matter out of court. After the matter was resolved, the land was secured for grazing by villagers from the five villages. While the men in the villages had failed to resolve the problem, the women and their more direct approach to the problem succeeded (Massay, 2019).

[60] # 5. Discussion and Conclusion

[61] Throughout this paper, first, it has become clear that regional and international voluntary land tenure guidelines’ development has influenced legislative making at the municipal level and the actions for social movements in Tanzania. International legal instruments on women’s rights coupled with the recent emergence of international and regional guidelines on land tenure and responsible land-based investments such as VGGTs have shaped and created space for local level bylaws and the national land policy development and revisions and capacity building aimed at addressing challenges affecting women’s participation in land decision making bodies.

[62] Secondly, the case studies outlined in the paper have demonstrated that rural women want to be recognized and to participate in decision making processes on land tenure, including through legally and socially legitimate processes such as the bylaws in Kisarawe. This in part has been sustained by the rights literacy programs implemented by Civil Society Organizations (CSOs). Women in Hanang district having realized that men could not solve their land access problems, mobilized themselves and took action to put pressure on men to act in defense of community land, a clear sign that women’s voices need to be taken onboard in land management and administration. At the sub-continental level, rural women have organized and made joint efforts to address challenges to their access, decision making and control of land. Examples include the1995 Beijing Conference and the Kilimanjaro Initiative of 2016 which resulted in a land rights charter outlining the women’s demands.

[63] Thirdly, the paper shows that the role of CSOs is moving beyond conventional training or advocacy work. With the growing land rights challenges, CSOs have claimed the space of demonstrating the need for change by developing national or local level model guides or laws that are inspired by international laws and best practices. The bylaws discussed in this paper were strongly influenced by the work of CSOs. In addition to those discussed in the previous section, there are other organizations have developed guides that are shaping actions on land and investment issues in many jurisdictions. Landesa, an international land rights organization, has developed country and context specific guidebooks for communities, investors and governments in Tanzania and in Ghana (Landesa, 2017). Namati, another international land rights organization, developed community-investor negotiation guides (Namati, 2018). All these guides underscore the importance of step by step processes to ensure women’s participation in land decision making processes.

[64] In conclusion, women’s rights to participate in decision making has long been a terrain of struggle and contestations. International human rights law and women’s rights movements across the globe have addressed and provided such rights. In relation to land decision making bodies, women’s struggles have even been more difficult because land is culturally considered to be owned by men. This cultural stereotype, has, to a large extent, been addressed by the progressive legal and constitutional provisions which guarantee equal land right between men and women. Despite the presence of progressive legal frameworks, implementation has been difficult, cultural norms persist, and there are still some gray areas in the laws as shown in the paper. The global land grab phenomena exacerbated the weaknesses in national legal systems and the emergence of international guides on land tenure and land-based investments have provided a space and opportunity to strengthen the national and local level land legislations as well as reinforce social movements seeking secure land rights for men and women. Innovations coming from CSOs have helped in enhancing women’s participation in decision making. These cases build on many other documented creative and powerful ways in which women negotiate, contest, resist and create room to exercise in their rights and amplify their struggles over land rights.
