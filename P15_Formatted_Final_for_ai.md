[0] # Guatemala: A Global Milestone and Pioneering Experience in VGGT Implementation. A Decade of Lessons for Building Responsible Land Governance (2012-2024).

[1] # Abstract

[2] Guatemala the first country to request technical assistance from FAO, marking a global milestone, driving the integration of Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure of Land, Fisheries and Forests in the Context of National Food Security (VGGT) into key national policies. The Guatemalan agrarian question, a legacy of incomplete reforms and historical inequality (Gini 0.84), sought transformation following the 1996 Peace Accords and the National Policy for Integral Rural Development (2009). Implementation of VGGT was structured into four phases, employing a multi-actor approach involving the government, indigenous peoples, peasants, civil society and private sector. The process achieved the formulation of an Agrarian Policy (2014) and tools development. This political commitment has seen both lights (initial institutional progress) and shadows (institutional volatility). In 2024, Guatemala emphasized past experiences as a lesson learned before Committee on World Food Security. This article examines progress and challenges, offering recommendations for an equitable and sustainable tenure policy relevant to 21st century agrarian reform.

[3] **Keywords:** Land governance, Guatemala, VGGT, Agrarian reform, Rural development, Food security, Agrarian conflict.

[4] # Introduction: A Pioneering Path

[5] In May 2012, a significant milestone for global land governance was reached with the adoption of the VGGT by the World Food Security Council (CFS). Almost simultaneously, in Guatemala, the government was reviewing and strengthening its agrarian policy framework, promulgating the implementation plan for the National Policy for Integral Rural Development (PNDRI).

[6] The PNDRI, approved in 2009 through Government Agreement 196-2009, was not just another policy; it was the product of a broad participatory process developed across different administrations and involving various social organizations. This policy recognized the critical importance of rural areas due to their challenges, shortcomings, and potential; it highlighted the country's multiculturalism as a driving force for national unity; and it was based on the premise that rural development is a fundamental pillar of national development, with a central role for the State. Furthermore, the PNDRI addressed the urgent need to overcome poverty, inequality, and exclusion as essential components of the common good, and it recognized that addressing the agrarian question and rural development represented the main shortcomings in the implementation of the Peace Accords. This participatory process had the merit of integrating the proposals of historically excluded sectors, including indigenous, peasant, labor, cooperative, small-scale rural producer, environmental, and academic organizations, as well as the Church, seeking to achieve national agreements on the challenges of comprehensive rural development. Despite having been subject to a constitutional challenge in 2012, the PNDRI was ratified by the Constitutional Court at the end of January 2013, consolidating its legal and political firmness.

[7] In this context of renewal and commitment to rural development, the National Council for Urban and Rural Development (CONADUR) requested the President of the Republic to effectively implement the PNDRI in May 2013. In response, on June 24, 2013, the Specific Cabinet for Comprehensive Rural Development (Government Agreement 262-2013) was legally established, chaired by the President of the Republic and composed of various ministries and key secretariats of the Presidency, including the Secretariat of Agrarian Affairs (SAA). The functions of this Cabinet included formulating the National Comprehensive Rural Development Plan, coordinating inter-institutional programs, and ensuring the efficient implementation of the PNDRI, empowering it to issue the necessary regulations. Within this framework of renewed institutionalization and the need for a thorough review of agrarian policy, the SAA, as the governing body for agrarian question and as part of this high-level Cabinet, drafted a new Agrarian Policy between 2012 and 2013. It was at this strategic moment, in May 2013, that the Presidential Commissioner for Rural Development encouraged the SAA to formally request technical assistance from the FAO Representation in Guatemala to understand and implement the VGGT. This action not only highlighted the country's commitment but also set an invaluable precedent by making Guatemala the first country globally to seek assistance for the implementation of these guidelines.

[8] The SAA's request triggered a series of decisive actions, including various technical assistance missions, extensive outreach and training processes on the VGGT, and rounds of consultations with different sectors to incorporate their principles into the nascent Agrarian Policy. The close collaboration between FAO and the SAA was fundamental, culminating in the formulation of a national Agrarian Policy deeply inspired by the voluntary guidelines. This policy was approved through Government Agreement 372-2014 in October 2014. This initial, albeit formal, step represented the starting point for a complex and multifaceted interaction between the principles of the VGGT and Guatemala's deeply rooted agrarian question, a legacy of historically incomplete reforms and persistent tensions.

[9] Guatemala's experience, from its initial application to the present day, stands as a crucial case study for understanding the complexities and potential of integrating international normative frameworks into specific national contexts. This article aims to offer an academic reflection on this trajectory, analyzing how the country, a pioneer in the application of the VGGT, has addressed them in order to tackle the agrarian question. As Gómez y Graziano (2025) explains, this issue should be understood as an unresolved structural problem that that differs from the agricultural question because the latter focuses on productivity, whereas the agrarian question centers on inequality in land tenure.

[10] This reflection is grounded in the insider knowledge of key actors[^1] directly involved in facilitating and documenting the VGGT implementation process in Guatemala (2012–2024), combined with a systematic triangulation of academic literature, legal frameworks, and policy records. We explicitly distinguish the reflexivity and positionality of these key figures from the broader array of stakeholders, including peasant organizations, indigenous peoples, rural women, and local communities (such as those in the Polochic valley). These stakeholders were central actors and co-constructors of the multi-stakeholder platforms, dialogue tables, and policy processes analyzed in this study; their perspectives and contributions inform our analysis through the qualitative evidence preserved in consensus documents, public declarations, and institutional records rather than primary field surveys for this specific piece. Our purpose is to critically examine how the VGGT principles have been adopted and operationalized, assessing their trajectory from high-level policy formulation, such as the Agrarian Policy, to concrete dialogues and actions on the ground.

[11] The specific objectives of this article include: i) describing the process and key actions that have characterized the implementation of the VGGT in Guatemala from 2012 to 2024; ii) analyzing the progress, persistent challenges, and the highlights and lowlights that have emerged from this pioneering experience, from global, regional, national, and multi-sectoral perspectives; iii) extracting valuable lessons learned and proposing concrete recommendations to strengthen land governance in Guatemala and other countries facing similar challenges; and iv) contributing to the global discussion on the need to rethink the agrarian question in the 21st century, positioning the Guatemalan experience as a benchmark in these discussions, including at global conferences. In reviewing this decade of efforts, the article aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the implementation of the VGGT and the challenges it faces, serving as a foundation and pointing toward a more equitable and sustainable path for land tenure management on a global scale.

[12] # Historical Context and Governance Framework

[13] Guatemala is a multiethnic and pluricultural nation, the most populous in Central America with 14.9 million inhabitants (INE, 2019, p. 3). It is characterized by profound socioeconomic disparities: 59.3% of the population lives in poverty, a figure that escalates to 79.2% among Indigenous communities (INE, 2015). With a Human Development Index (HDI) of 0.663 in 2019, the country faces one of the most critical situations in Latin America (UNDP, 2022, p. 45), where chronic malnutrition affects 49% of children under five, the highest rate in the hemisphere (UNDP, 2016, p. 181).

[14] In this setting, the agrarian question is not merely a contemporary challenge but a deeply rooted historical legacy that has shaped the country's social, economic, and political fabric. The internal armed conflict that devastated the country between 1960 and 1996 had as one of its fundamental causes the extreme inequality in land distribution. The Commission for Historical Clarification (CEH) documented that most human rights violations occurred precisely in regions characterized by pronounced inequality in land tenure and the precariousness of peasant property, disproportionately affecting Indigenous communities and families (Castillo, 2015). This inequality was reflected in one of the highest Gini coefficients for land concentration worldwide, reaching 0.84, according to the 2003 Agrarian Census (INE, 2004, p. 19; SAA, 2014, p. 20), surpassing its Central American neighbors like El Salvador (0.81), Nicaragua (0.72), and Costa Rica (0.67). This same census revealed a concentration of land ownership where just 1.86% of landowners controlled 56.59% of arable land, while 43.3% of the highest-quality soils were dedicated to industrial export crops like sugarcane and oil palm, often at the expense of local food security (INE, 2004; UNDP, 2016, p. 181).

[15] The signing of the Peace Accords in 1996 not only sealed the end of the armed conflict but also explicitly recognized the agrarian question as a structural cause of the conflict and an indispensable element for building lasting peace and development. The Agreement on Socioeconomic Aspects and the Agrarian Situation (ASSA) established a clear mandate for reforming the legal framework for agriculture and for institutional development in rural areas, seeking to end the lack of protection and displacement of peasants and indigenous people, promote their full integration into the national economy, and regulate the sustainable use of land (Gómez, 2015, p. 83). This Agreement, with the status of a state commitment according to Decree 52-2005 (Framework Law of the Peace Accords), laid the foundation for the creation of post-conflict agrarian institutions.

[16] In compliance with these guidelines, several key institutions were created. The National Land Fund (FONTIERRAS) was established in 1999 (Decree 24-99 of the Congress of the Republic), with the objective of promoting peasants' access to land ownership through credit assistance and the encouragement of savings. Its allocation policy prioritized organized peasants, and a significant milestone was the explicit definition of women's access to land in its Article 21, a significant advance in Guatemala's agrarian legal history (Gómez, 2015, p. 84). In 1997, the Presidential Office for Land Conflict Resolution (CONTIERRA) was created to address agrarian conflicts, although, failing to achieve sufficient institutional weight, its functions were integrated into the Secretariat of Agrarian Affairs of the Presidency (SAA) in 2005 (Gómez, 2015, p. 84). Finally, in 2005, the Cadastral Information Registry (RIC) was established through Decree 41-2005, seeking a decentralized and efficient land registry and cadastral system, as demanded by the ASSA. (Gómez, 2015, p. 85).

[17] However, by 2013, agrarian institutions remained fragmented and of limited effectiveness. Despite the mandates of the Peace Accords to strengthen the state, post-1996 government policies, influenced by the Washington Consensus and structural adjustment, focused on reducing and privatizing state services, weakening institutional capacity in the agrarian sector and rural development (Gómez, 2015, p. 86). Market-based land access programs, the main mechanism for acquisition, had proven insufficient, as evidenced by the Land Fund's zero purchases of farms in 2012 (SAA, 2014). The low participation of women in land access programs (10.7% of loan beneficiaries between 1998 and 2014) and the persistence of patriarchal mechanisms continued to be structural barriers (Castillo, 2015, p. 126; FGT, 2016). The lack of clarity in ancestral rights and the registration of communal lands continued to be sources of conflict, with more than 1,300 active agrarian conflicts in the country (SAA, 2014). Furthermore, high tenure insecurity (33.22% of Guatemalans feared losing their land rights, according to the Global Property Rights Index; Land Portal, 2020) highlighted the ongoing need for robust legal frameworks.

[18] In this context of institutional efforts with partial results and persistent conflict, inequality and food insecurity remained immense challenges. According to a 2017 government report on SDG 5.a, which aims to achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls, access to land ownership and control for women is a transversal issue reflecting deep-seated inequalities. The report highlights that despite legal and organizational frameworks, efforts to incorporate gender equality in land access programs have been minimal and have not tangibly changed women's quality of life in rural areas. The PNDRI, approved in 2009 and ratified by the Constitutional Court in 2013 after a constitutional challenge, represented a renewed attempt to address these problems. The PNDRI, which prioritized the peasant economy (Gómez, 2015, p. 88), raised the need to promote sectoral policies, with the Agrarian Policy being the first of these, aiming to review and propose innovative ways of accessing productive assets, including land, with an emphasis on transparency and prioritizing women (Gómez, 2015, p. 89). It was in this scenario of a new political will to activate the PNDRI and the need for robust and internationally standardized tools, that the Secretariat of Agrarian Affairs of Guatemala was prompted to request, in May 2013, technical assistance from the FAO for the implementation of the Voluntary Guidelines, marking a turning point in the search for more equitable and sustainable land governance.

[19] # The FAO Request: A Global Milestone

[20] The Guatemalan agrarian context, as previously discussed, was characterized by its contentious nature, with structural origins and generating significant political, economic, and social tensions (Gómez, 2015). Faced with this reality, and at a crucial moment of institutional renewal following the ratification of the PNDRI in January 2013 and the drafting of a new Agrarian Policy by the Secretariat of Agrarian Affairs (SAA) between 2012 and 2013, the Guatemalan government actively sought tools and partners to address these challenges. It was at this juncture that the VGGT adopted globally in May 2012 by the Council for World Food Security (CFS), emerged as a particularly relevant instrument.

[21] In May 2013, the SAA, in its role as the governing body on agrarian question and with the support of the newly formed Specific Cabinet for Comprehensive Rural Development, formally requested technical assistance from the FAO Representation in the country to understand and implement the VGGT. This request was not a mere formality; it was based on a series of clear strategic motivations (Gómez, 2015), but above all, it represented a high-level political decision, a presidential decision agreed upon by the President of the Republic and the Secretary of the Cabinet for Rural Development. The fundamental purpose was to create a political environment that would allow them to develop an Agrarian Policy; in the words of Zapata (personal communication, December 22, 2025) “a framework to confront the prejudices and anti-establishment sentiments surrounding agrarian question in the country”. First, there was an explicit need for technical support to reach consensus on the formulation of the Agrarian Policy, recognizing FAO's extensive experience. Second, it was understood that poor land governance was a fundamental problem hindering rural development and directly affecting the food and nutrition security of the Guatemalan population, and the VGGT offered a path to reducing hunger and poverty. Finally, FAO had a relatively new but universally approved international instrument specifically designed to support countries in this area (Gómez, 2015).

[22] Guatemala's proactive approach in being the first country in the world to request technical assistance from the FAO for the implementation of the VGGT marked a global milestone and set an invaluable precedent (Mabikke *et al.*, 2022). This initiative demonstrated a remarkable ability to identify and capitalize on a unique political juncture: the high relevance of the Guidelines' content to the complex national agrarian reality, its alignment with the draft Agrarian Policy under development, and the political opportunity to promote agrarian regulations backed by an international instrument agreed upon by the global community (Gómez, 2015). The neutrality provided by the FAO proved crucial, fostering an environment of trust essential for the implementation and dialogue process to advance and solidify.

[23] The immediate implication of this Guatemalan leadership was the integration of the VGGTs into the FAO. From the FAO's technical perspective, this milestone was not just an administrative act, but the beginning of what Castañeda (2023) defines as 'Phase I: Institutional Architecture'. During this period (2012-2013), technical assistance focused on positioning the FAO as a neutral mediator, allowing the VGGT to serve as a technical bridge between the government's vision and the demands of social organizations, under a social learning approach that sought to rebuild institutional trust.

[24] The country's new land governance policy, which in turn formed part of the National Policy for Comprehensive Rural Development (Mabikke *et al.*, 2022), extended its approach to recognizing and strengthening Indigenous communal land tenure and management systems, as well as promoting women's land rights. The process aimed not only to improve access to land but also to contribute to the development of the rural economy and its full integration into the broader context.

[25] # Implementation of VGGT: from narrative to change

[26] Guatemala’s pioneering request to the FAO in 2013 was not merely a statement of intent; it quickly translated into an active and multifaceted process of implementing the VGGT that transcended mere rhetoric to generate tangible changes in land governance. The transition from the VGGT’s global principles to institutional practice in Guatemala was not an inertial process; it was a planned strategy in four distinct phases, initially systematized by FAO technical assistance (Gamboa, 2013) and subsequently analyzed under the Working with People (WWP) metamodel by Castañeda (2023) and it is conceptualized as a social planning metamodel that connects knowledge and action through shared learning, balancing ethical, technical, and political dimensions (Cazorla and De Los Ríos, 2012).

[27] This critical path allowed the Guidelines to move beyond an aspirational framework and become effective tools for public management and social dialogue.

[28] # The Architecture of Participation: A Multi-Actor and Multi-Level Approach

[29] The success of implementing the VGGT in Guatemala stemmed from a pluralistic approach that broke with the historical fragmentation of the agrarian sector. As documented in the systematization of technical assistance (Gamboa, 2013), a support framework was established that ensured genuine national representation, bringing together diverse sectors and stakeholders. This multi-stakeholder and multi-level architecture included:

[30] Government Sector and Autonomous Entities: Led by the Secretariat of Agrarian Affairs (SAA) as the governing body, with the strategic participation of the FONTIERRAS and the RIC, along with the Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock and Food (MAGA) and the National Council for Urban and Rural Development (CONADUR). Their involvement was crucial for the formulation of the Agrarian Policy and the integration of the VGGT into its operational instruments (FAO, 2017).

[31] Peasant and Indigenous Organizations: Actors such as the Coordinator of Indigenous and Peasant Organizations (CONIC), the Agrarian Platform, and representatives of ancestral authorities played a fundamental role in ensuring that the rights-based approach and the Indigenous worldview permeated the technical discussion. Their participation was essential to contextualize the VGGT to local realities and guarantee cultural relevance.

[32] Private and Productive Sector: A milestone in the process was the inclusion of the Chamber of Agriculture and the cooperative sector, which allowed for technical dialogue in a neutral space facilitated by the FAO (Gómez, 2015). This interaction broadened the legitimacy and viability of the proposals.

[33] Civil Society and Academia: Organizations such as CONGCOOP and Action Aid, together with universities, contributed a critical vision and academic rigor to the policy proposal, enriching the analysis and the quality of the interventions.

[34] Local Level: Implementation transcended the central level, reaching territories and communities. This involved the direct participation of individuals and legal organizations affected by agrarian conflict, bringing the principles of VGGT to the level of the plot and community management.

[35] # The Four Phases of the Critical Path

[36] According to the analysis by Gamboa (2013) and Castañeda (2023), the process was consolidated through four interconnected phases:

[37] Phase I: Preparation and High-Level Dialogue. An initial Coordinating Team was established between the SAA and the FAO, which applied the ethical-social dimension of the WWP metamodel to legitimize VGGT as a common language between sectors with historically antagonistic views. This phase focused on building trust and identifying shared interests to initiate the dialogue process.

[38] Phase II: Context Analysis and Alignment. This phase involved an in-depth diagnostic process to adapt the Guidelines to the Guatemalan context, aligning them with the PNDRI and identifying regulatory gaps in current agrarian legislation. Studies and consultations were conducted to adapt the global principles of the Guidelines to the socioeconomic, cultural, and legal specificities of Guatemala.

[39] Phase III: Capacity Building and Territorial Dissemination. This was the driving force behind social learning and empowerment. The competencies of 223 key actors were strengthened through intensive training and workshops (Castañeda, 2023). Communication for development was crucial for Indigenous communities to adopt the VGGT as a tool for defending their communal land tenure systems, using culturally relevant methods and materials, including radio programs in Mayan languages.

[40] Phase IV: Methodological Institutionalization. This phase culminated in the creation and integration of specific technical instruments into institutional operations. This included incorporating the VGGT, model into FONTIERRAS for the development of Sustainable Agrarian Communities[^2] (CAS), validating the Open Tenure tool for participatory mapping and registration of tenure rights (ILC, 2023), and strengthening the System for Addressing Agrarian Conflicts (SARTCA) (Castañeda, 2023). This approach enabled technical assistance to support the reformulation of the Agrarian Policy within the framework of the PNDRI and the restructuring of the CAS model, as well as the development of a specific policy to facilitate women's access to land, and the dialogue for the communal land regulations of the RIC and efforts were bolstered by South-South cooperation, notably an exchange with Brazil’s INCRA, which provided technical insights into cadastral systems and land regularization policies (FAO, 2019)

[41] Furthermore, the focus on gender equity moved beyond discourse with the creation of the operational manual for the Policy to Facilitate Access to Land and Productive Assets for Peasant Women. This instrument, supported by a baseline study, was developed using the VGGT Technical Guide on Governing Land for Women and Men to establish institutional mechanisms that ensure the visibility of women’s participation in Maya, Xinka, Garifuna, and Mestiza communities. (FAO, 2019).

[42] This phased structure and broad participation ensured that the process was not merely a consultation, but a collaborative space where political commitment translated into established technical capacities and operational tools. The Guatemalan experience demonstrates that responsible land governance requires this balance: neutral technical mediation, approaches such as WWP, and institutionalization that transcends political narratives to generate lasting change.

[43] # Intertwining Stories: Practical Cases in the Application of VGGT

[44] The implementation of the VGGT in Guatemala, through the Agrarian Policy and FAO technical assistance, has resulted in practical and emblematic cases that illustrate both institutional progress and persistent challenges in building responsible land governance. These examples demonstrate the complexity of adapting global principles to local realities and the importance of a multi-stakeholder approach that prioritizes equity and sustainability, with the technical guidelines derived from the VGGTs themselves being fundamental tools in this practical application.

[45] A key model in this trajectory has been the restructuring and promotion of CAS by the FONTIERRAS, a process that began in 2012. This collaboration enabled the development of methodological manuals that incorporated principles of equity, cultural relevance, and social justice for land access and productive development and study cases to improve the program.

[46] [SPECIAL] However, while the promotion of women's land rights is a cross-cutting theme in the implementation of gender equality and is reflected in the Agrarian Policy (SAA, 2014) and the specific policy of the FONTIERRAS (approved in 2016), a significant gap remains. Studies, such as the one by the Guillermo Toriello Foundation (FGT, 2016), reveal that, historically, women have represented only 11% of the beneficiaries of land access programs. Despite regulatory advances, obstacles persist, such as high notary fees for co-ownership and deeply rooted patriarchal patterns within peasant organizations (FGT, 2016). Nevertheless, the documentation of good practices of governance[^3] by the FAO and the University of San Carlos (USAC) has brought their role to the forefront.

[47] [SPECIAL] The complex reality of communal lands and their historical vulnerability have also found a guiding framework in the VGGT. The Group for the Promotion of Communal Lands (GPTC) and the RIC worked to secure recognition of these lands, including specific regulations for the cadastral process based on ILO Convention 169 and the Peace Accords. In this context was launched the initiative of Communication for Development[^4] aimed at informing and documenting relevant experiences in governance and recognition of communal lands with an emphasis on customary practices and uses.

[48] The application of the VGGT has provided an essential normative and moral framework for addressing historical and highly sensitive agrarian conflicts, including those with transitional justice implications. Strengthening the System for Attention to Agrarian Conflicts (SARTCA) under the VGGT framework (Castañeda, 2023) seeks, in line with Principle 24 of the Guidelines, to establish more transparent and equitable resolution pathways, replacing the use of force with technical dialogue. Cases the conflict-ridden regions of the Polochic Valley and Ixil saw their demands for justice and governance strengthened. In the Polochic Valley, FAO Guatemala has directly applied the VGGT in projects funded by the United Nations Peacebuilding Fund (PBF). The project, carried out in partnership with the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR), this initiative strengthens institutional and community capacities for governance and multi-stakeholder dialogue, explicitly integrating human rights, gender, and the 'triple nexus' (peace, development, and humanitarian aid). While it promotes alternative dispute resolution and monitors Inter-American Commission on Human Rights (IACHR) precautionary measures, the State’s response remains often insufficient or detached from the reality faced by rural population.

[49] Finally, technological empowerment has emerged as a strategic ally. The implementation of the Open Tenure tool has allowed rural communities to document their tenure rights in a participatory manner, strengthening legal certainty and combating informality (Castañeda, 2023 and ILC, 2023). This exercise not only facilitates registration but also empowers communities against dispossession, aligning technical innovation with the principles of inclusion and transparency of the Guidelines.

[50] In sum, the cases presented illustrate how the principles of gender equality and responsible land governance have begun to transform Guatemala’s agrarian landscape by introducing specific elements of the VGGT. Although progress has been made in closing institutional and technological gaps, the lack of recent official data on progress and results toward achieving responsible land tenure governance remains a significant hurdle. Nevertheless, anecdotal data and some empirical evidence suggests that while responsible governance has advanced in specific cases, structural inequalities persist, particularly for indigenous women, underscoring that the consolidation of a truly equitable governance system remains an ongoing task. These practical cases are provided to as a bridge between a decade of implementation and the current debates and how local experiences can be useful and taken into account in the development of global standards on governance of land tenure and human rights.

[51] # Reflections on Political Commitment: Progress and Setbacks

[52] Guatemala's political commitment to implementing the VGGT (2012-2024) has been a dynamic process, characterized by periods of clear political will (lights) and significant setbacks (shadows), reflecting the inherent complexity of the agrarian question and the country's institutional fragility. This balance between progress and stagnation is crucial for understanding the construction of responsible land governance.

[53] # The Lights of Commitment: An Initial and Collaborative Impulse

[54] The initial political will was evident from the pioneering request to the FAO in 2013, which marked a turning point and generated significant momentum. This commitment materialized in the adoption of the Agrarian Policy in 2014, which, directly inspired by the VGGT, became a key instrument for guiding government action on land tenure. This achievement was significant, as it represented the culmination of years of dialogue and consensus-building among diverse stakeholders. By establishing clear strategic axes on land access, resolution of agrarian conflicts, legal certainty and security, and access to other productive assets, the Agrarian Policy laid the groundwork for the implementation of the VGGT.

[55] Furthermore, political commitment was reflected in technical support and the strengthening of key institutions such as the FONTIERRAS and the RIC, where the principles of gender equality were integrated to improve access to land and legal security. The active role of civil society organizations, along with international cooperation, acted as catalysts and co-creators of this agenda. The Special Cabinet for Comprehensive Rural Development, to which the SAA belonged, was a high-level body that provided the necessary political framework to promote these initiatives (Gómez, 2015). This initial phase demonstrated that, with clear political direction and external technical support, it was possible to generate substantial progress toward more equitable governance. And currently dialogue efforts with the Presidential Commission for Peace and Human Rights (COPADEH).

[56] # The Shadows of Institutional Volatility: Setbacks and Fragmentation

[57] However, the path toward responsible agrarian governance has been fraught with setbacks, highlighting what academia identifies as marked “institutional volatility” (Castañeda, 2023). The most significant blow to the established institutional framework was the elimination of the Secretariat of Agrarian Affairs (SAA) in 2020. This action represented a “systemic setback” that fractured a decade of efforts, dismantling the governing body for agrarian question and one of the main technical interlocutors with the FAO and civil society (Castañeda, 2023). The SAA, which had been instrumental in formulating the Agrarian Policy and promoting gender equality, was dissolved, resulting in a fragmentation of functions and an invaluable loss of institutional memory and coordination capacity.

[58] The elimination of the SAA and the lack of long-term legal protection for multi-stakeholder platforms allowed political will to erode with each change in administration, affecting the continuity of the Agrarian Policy approved in 2014 (Castañeda, 2023; Gómez, 2015). The effective implementation of the PNDRI was also delayed or weakened by the lack of strong institutional leadership. Adding to these complexities was the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, which exacerbated existing inequalities and diverted resources and attention from ongoing land governance actions, particularly affecting rural women (FAO *et al.*, 2022). This combination of factors demonstrated the fragility of progress when it is not rooted in a robust and politically supported long-term institutional framework.

[59] # A New Horizon: The 2024 Agrarian Agreement

[60] Despite setbacks, Guatemala's journey toward equitable land governance seems to have found renewed hope in the agreement between President-elect Bernardo Arévalo and peasant organizations. The agreement signifies a political commitment to addressing institutional shortcomings and weaknesses that characterized periods of uncertainty.

[61] One of the agreement's most significant provisions is the commitment to create a mechanism to address land tenure conflicts. Additionally, the agreement emphasizes the strengthening of institutions related to land tenure. The 2024 agreement addresses the unfulfilled mandates of the 1996 peace accords and aligns with the principles of the global validity of VGGT by emphasizing the participation of peasant and indigenous organizations and recognizing collective rights.

[62] Ultimately, Guatemala's experience illustrates that political commitment is the cornerstone of VGGT implementation. The friction between initial resolve, institutional inertia, and periods of renewed momentum defines the complexity of establishing responsible land governance within a vulnerable institutional framework. While the shadows of volatility underscore the urgent need for legal safeguards and sustained engagement, the light of recent agreements signals the potential for a new cycle of progress in building more equitable and sustainable governance.

[63] # Conclusion: from global pioneering to institutional resilience

[64] Guatemala's journey as the first country to formally adopt the VGGT offers a profound lesson in institutional resilience. The evolution from the initial request in 2013 to the present day demonstrates that land governance is an ongoing process of negotiation and political compromise.

[65] Despite periods of institutional instability, particularly the dissolution of the Secretariat of Agrarian Affairs in 2020, the principles of the VGGT have endured thanks to tangible instruments. As highlighted during the 52nd plenary session of the Committee on World Food Security (CFS 52) in October 2024, Guatemala has demonstrated its commitment to advancing these principles. Over the years, it has adopted various guidelines on food security, land governance, and agri-food systems.

[66] In conclusion, Guatemala's pioneering decade (2012–2024) demonstrates that the VGGT are robust instrument for addressing the structural causes of agrarian conflict in the 21st century. The combination of initial political leadership, the technical mediation, and the use of multi-stakeholder methodologies such as the meta-model offers a clear roadmap for the international community.

[67] The approach to the agrarian question must be comprehensive, multidimensional, and institutionally protected, moving beyond simple land redistribution toward integrated rural development. Guatemala’s trajectory as a pioneer in application the VGGT demonstrates how countries in the global south, while continuing to engage in international development cooperation, can transition from passive recipients of technical assistance to active generators of policy innovation and institutional learning for global tenure governance. This experience illustrates concrete measures for institutionalizing principles within national frameworks to promote responsible land governance, reaffirming that addressing land issues through the secure land tenure, justice, and peace bestows dignity upon rural populations.
